Devotional

Yesterday we lay down in a bed too short, under a covering too narrow, and found no rest there. Today Isaiah explains why. The storm that kept passing through that bed was not bad luck. It was God Himself, risen — doing a kind of work His own people called strange.

Here is the passage, Isaiah 28:21-22, from the ESV: "For the LORD will rise up as on Mount Perazim; as in the Valley of Gibeon he will be roused; to do his deed—strange is his deed! and to work his work—alien is his work! Now therefore do not scoff, lest your bonds be made strong; for I have heard a decree of destruction from the Lord GOD of hosts against the whole land."

Those two names are battlefields, and every hearer knew them. At Perazim, David watched God burst through the Philistines like a flood through a broken dam, and said so in 2 Samuel chapter 5. At Gibeon, in Joshua chapter 10, God fought for Israel so openly that the sun itself seemed to stand still while He finished it. Perazim and Gibeon mean one thing in Israel's memory: God rising up for His people, against their enemies.

That is why this verse lands like a blow. God will rise as He did at Perazim — but this time He rises against Jerusalem. The hands that once broke their enemies now measure their wall, annul their covenant, and sweep their shelter away. Same God. Same power. Turned, by their own refusal, in the opposite direction.

The key word is zar, the Hebrew word for strange, foreign, alien — something that does not belong where it stands. Isaiah uses it twice: strange is His deed, alien is His work. Judgment is strange work for God, not because it is against His holiness — He is never less than just — but because it is not the work He delights to do to His own. A father disciplining a son is doing real father-work, but nobody who knows him would call it the work he loves. He has to be roused to it. And notice who roused Him here: not Assyria. Jerusalem did, by scoffing at His cornerstone and signing with death instead.

I will be honest: this verse used to keep me up. A God who can be against His own people is not a comfortable God. But sit with the alternative — a God who watches His people sign with death and says nothing, and lets the leaning wall fall. That is not mercy. That is abandonment. The strange work is love telling the truth early, while there is yet a stone to flee to.

Here is the landing for a Monday morning: "Do not scoff, lest your bonds be made strong." Scoffing does not loosen the chains. It tightens them — one notch every time we laugh off a clear word. But the stone is still laid in Zion, and whoever believes in Him will not be put to shame. God's strange work has a purpose: to drive us off every other foundation and onto that one. If some hard thing right now has the feel of God being against you, do not scoff, and do not run to Egypt. Ask first: is He tearing down a shelter of lies to get you back to the Stone? If He is, then even this is mercy wearing a stern face — and the Stone still holds.

Lord, when Your work feels strange to us, keep us from scoffing; loosen our bonds, tear down our false shelters, and set us again on Christ, Your tested stone. Amen.

From The Ancient World

Sometimes a legendary monastery stays legend because nobody can find it. On a small island in Scotland, the local people went looking for theirs — and found it.

The Isle of Lismore sits in the Inner Hebrides, home to only about 170 people. Tradition has long said that Saint Mo Luóc — Moluag — founded a monastery there in the sixth century, one of the early centers that carried Christianity through northern Britain. But apart from written records and a broken cross, there was almost no physical evidence, and scholars could not say how big or important it really was.

This month, that changed. In the October 2026 issue of the journal Antiquity, Clare Ellis of Argyll Archaeology and her colleagues published a community-led excavation begun by the Lismore Historical Society in 2016 — island volunteers, crowd-funded digs, working around their thirteenth-century parish church. They uncovered an early medieval cemetery, burials radiocarbon dated between A.D. 650 and 1040, fragments of carved cross slabs, and an oval building filled with traces of skilled craftwork.

The real surprise was the metal. The team found more than 1,000 pieces of metalworking debris — crucibles, lids, and molds used to cast penannular brooches — with traces of copper, silver, and even gold. That is not the footprint of a lonely hermitage. That is a wealthy, working center of worship, craft, and learning. Gordon Noble of the University of Aberdeen, a co-author, says its importance may have rivaled the best-known monasteries in early medieval Britain — Iona, founded by Columba in A.D. 563, among them. The team is careful, and so should we be: no direct evidence from Mo Luóc's own sixth century has turned up yet. What they have is the thriving community that followed in his steps.

Why does it matter to us? Because the faith spread on islands like this — not only through councils and kings, but through small communities who buried their dead in hope, carved the cross in stone, and did excellent work with their hands. And it was recovered the fitting way: not by a great institution swooping in, but by the island's own people, digging patiently for the faith handed down to them.

Source: Smithsonian Magazine, October 9, 2026, reporting Ellis and colleagues in Antiquity, October 2026; also reported by Scientific American, October 1, 2026, and Heritage Daily, October 15, 2026.

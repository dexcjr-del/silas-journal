The Daily Read — Monday, November 9

Strong Meat — The Daily Read · November 9, 2026 · by Silas · 1,034 words · plain text

Devotional

Yesterday we gazed at the King in His beauty and at a city turned untroubled, a tent whose stakes would never be pulled up. Today Isaiah finishes the chapter, and he finishes it strangely — with a river where no river runs, and a ship that never sails.

Isaiah 33:21-24, from the ESV: "But there the LORD in majesty will be for us a place of broad rivers and streams, from which no galley with oars can go, nor majestic ship can pass. For the LORD is our judge; the LORD is our lawgiver; the LORD is our king; he will save us. Your cords hang loose; they cannot hold the mast firm in its place, nor keep the sail spread out. Then prey and spoil in abundance will be divided; even the lame will take the prey. And no inhabitant will say, 'I am sick'; the people who dwell there will be forgiven their iniquity."

Jerusalem has no river. Every great city of the ancient world sat on water — Babylon on the Euphrates, Egypt on the Nile — and rivers meant trade, and rivers meant invasion, because the same water that carried merchants carried warships. Isaiah says God Himself will be Jerusalem's river: all the blessing of broad waters, with none of the danger. No galley with oars comes up this river. No majestic ship passes. The LORD in majesty replaces the majestic ship — same word, and Isaiah means us to feel the swap.

Then verse 23 turns, and it sounds like bad news. Your cords hang loose. The mast will not stand. The sail will not spread. Most readers take this as Zion's own ship, broken and useless — and that is exactly the point. The victory at the end of this chapter is not won by a city that finally got its rigging right. The spoil is divided anyway. Even the lame take the prey. People who could not march, could not fight, could not even run to the battlefield come home carrying plunder, because the battle was never theirs to win.

The key word is nasa — forgiven, in "the people who dwell there will be forgiven their iniquity." Nasa means to lift, to carry, to bear away. It is the word for the scapegoat's cousin in the wilderness, and Isaiah will use it again at the crossroads of his book: "Surely he has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows" (Isaiah 53:4). Forgiven, in Isaiah's mouth, is not God deciding to overlook the weight. It is God picking the weight up and carrying it off Himself. That is why no inhabitant says, "I am sick." The deepest sickness of the city was never fever. It was iniquity — and iniquity, carried away, takes the sickness with it.

Micah ends his whole book on this same shore: "Who is a God like you, pardoning iniquity and passing over transgression for the remnant of his inheritance?" (Micah 7:18). Pardon is the signature of this God. Judges and lawgivers and kings are everywhere in the ancient world. A King who is all three, and who carries His people's sin away besides — there is only One.

Here is the landing for a Monday. Some of us start this week like that ship: cords loose, mast wobbling, sail we cannot spread. Isaiah does not tell us to re-rig before God moves. The LORD is our judge, our lawgiver, our king — present tense, before we have fixed a single rope — and He will save us. Even the lame take the prey. Walk into this week as a dweller, not a sailor: forgiven first, carried always, and surprised by spoil you did not earn.

Lord, be our river and our King today; carry away our iniquity as You promised, and let even the lame among us come home with the prey. Amen.

From The Ancient World

Out in the Judean Desert, about ten miles east of Jerusalem, a fortress became a monastery — and this month it gave up a little of its gold.

Fox News Digital reported this month that Israel's Coordinator of Government Activities in the Territories (COGAT) announced finds at Hyrcania, in the northern Judean Desert. The site began in the late Hellenistic period, in the second or first century B.C., as a fortress. In the Byzantine period, as monks filled the Judean Desert, a Christian monastery was established there, attributed to the activity of Saint Sabbas, one of the founders of desert monasticism, and it operated for hundreds of years.

The finds themselves are small and stubborn: two gold coins and a delicate gold ring, still there after some 1,400 years at a site long vulnerable to looting. The coins are solidi bearing the image of the Emperor Heraclius, who ruled the Byzantine Empire from A.D. 610 to 641. COGAT said the finds "attest to Christian activity in the region during the Byzantine era," and that they reinforce the picture of Hyrcania as part of the monastic landscape of the Judean Desert in that period.

Archaeologist Binyamin Har-Even of the Civil Administration's Archaeology Unit said the artifacts "reflect an important chapter from the Byzantine period and the early Christian tradition in the region," and spoke of the unit's work to protect the past and make it accessible to everyone.

Sit with what those coins meant where they were found. Desert monks were men who had walked away from money on purpose — out past Jerusalem, into the dry hills, to pray where nobody could watch them do it. Yet even there, the empire's gold passed through their hands, because monasteries fed travelers, ransomed captives, and kept lamps lit. Two coins and a ring are not a treasure hoard. They are closer to a day's evidence that the quiet life in the desert was real, worked, and lasted centuries — long after the fortress it replaced had stopped mattering. The monks of Hyrcania would have liked Isaiah's math: a stronghold is not what keeps a place. God is.

Source: Fox News Digital, November 2026, reporting the announcement by Israel's Coordinator of Government Activities in the Territories (COGAT) and archaeologist Binyamin Har-Even on the Hyrcania excavations.

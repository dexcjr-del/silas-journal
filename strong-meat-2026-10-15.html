Devotional

For two days now we have been sitting in Isaiah 28, listening to a room full of drunk priests laugh at a prophet. They mocked his simple teaching — precept upon precept, line upon line — as baby talk. Yesterday God answered their laughter with a foreign tongue. Today we come to the saddest line in the whole exchange, because buried in the middle of the judgment is an offer. And it is an offer they had already turned down.

Here is the verse, Isaiah 28:12, from the ESV: "This is rest; give rest to the weary; and this is repose; yet they would not hear."

Read that last part again and let it sit. Yet they would not hear. Not could not. Would not.

Picture the scene one more time. Jerusalem is tired. Assyria is moving, alliances are failing, everybody is running — to Egypt for horses, to the wine cup for relief, to political deals for safety. Into all that running, God speaks one calm sentence: stop, and let the weary rest. He was not asking them to do more. He was offering to carry what their running never could.

The key word is the Hebrew word for rest, menuchah. It comes from a root that means to settle down, to cease, to come to a stop in a place where you can finally stay. It is the same word family used when the ark came to rest, and when Ruth was told that Boaz would find her a resting place. Menuchah is not a nap. It is not collapsing because you cannot go another step. It is settled rest — the kind that only comes when somebody trustworthy says, "You can put the weight down now. It will still be held in the morning."

That is what God offered His people. And they said no.

Why would tired people refuse rest? I have wrestled with that one, and I think the answer is uncomfortable: rest requires trust, and striving only requires motion. As long as we are moving, we can pretend we are in control. Rest admits we are not. So we keep the calendar full, keep the news on, keep rehearsing the problem at 2 a.m., and we call that being responsible. Isaiah's people called it survival. God called it refusal.

Now watch where Scripture takes this. Centuries later, a Galilean teacher stands up and makes the same offer in His own voice. In Matthew chapter 11, Jesus says, "Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest." Friends, that is Isaiah 28:12 come back around — only this time the rest is not a policy or a place. It is a Person. What Jerusalem refused from a prophet, Jesus offers from Himself. And notice: the offer did not expire when they refused it. God is patient that way. He keeps setting the same table.

But here is the landing, and I will not soften it: the offer still has to be received, and receiving looks like stopping. Not quitting your responsibilities. Not pretending the hard things are not hard. It means you take one real pause today — before the phone, before the running — and you let God be God for five quiet minutes over His Word. The weary do not need a better plan this morning. We need to finally hear the sentence we have been outrunning: this is rest.

Lord, we confess that we would rather run than trust; teach our weary hearts to stop, to hear You, and to rest in Christ today. Amen.

From The Ancient World

Sometimes archaeology hands you a headline. This week it handed us plumbing — and it is better than it sounds.

On October 11, the Israel Antiquities Authority announced that excavations ahead of a new residential development south of Kibbutz Ramat Rachel, near Jerusalem, have uncovered a roughly 984-foot section of the ancient Lower Aqueduct. According to Fox News, reporting the Authority's announcement on October 15, the waterway once ran about 13 miles from Solomon's Pools, south of Bethlehem, all the way to the Temple Mount in Jerusalem.

The aqueduct was first built in the time of the Hasmonean kings, before the time of Jesus, and it stayed in intermittent use, remarkably, until about a century ago. Its engineering is the quiet marvel here. The whole system ran on gravity alone, dropping only about three feet of elevation per mile — a slope so gentle the water barely knew it was moving, yet it arrived, day after day, at the Second Temple. There it was used for washing and for the ritual purification that Temple worship required.

Why does a water channel matter to folks like us? Because it fills in the ordinary world behind the words we read. When the Gospels show us Jesus walking the Temple courts, this is part of how that place functioned — real water, carried real miles, so real people could worship. One scholar quoted in the report, Douglas Estes of New College of Florida, noted his confidence that Jesus and His disciples would have known water supplied by this very system, though he was careful — and we should be too — to say no artifact can prove an individual's use of it.

And I cannot leave this without saying it plain: the water came from Bethlehem to Jerusalem so that cleansing could happen at the Temple. Christians have long called Jesus Himself the living water, born in Bethlehem, who became the true Temple and offered Himself once for all. The stones do not preach that sermon. But they sure do set the stage for it.

The aqueduct section will be preserved and built into the entrance of the new development, so visitors will be able to stand beside it. Two thousand years old, and still teaching — line upon line, you might say.

Source: Israel Antiquities Authority announcement, October 11, as reported by Fox News, October 15, 2026.

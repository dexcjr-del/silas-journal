Devotional

Yesterday we closed Isaiah 28 out in a farmer's field, watching God thresh grain and stop the wheel in time. Today we turn the page to chapter 29. Jerusalem has a new name — Ariel — and God says He will encamp against her Himself and bring her low. But first He looks inside the walls, and what He finds is not crude idolatry. It is worship. Busy, fluent, regular worship.

Here it is, Isaiah 29:13-14, from the ESV: "And the Lord said: 'Because this people draw near with their mouth and honor me with their lips, while their hearts are far from me, and their fear of me is a commandment taught by men, therefore, behold, I will again do wonderful things with this people, with wonder upon wonder; and the wisdom of their wise men shall perish, and the discernment of their discerning men shall be hidden.'"

Picture it. Mouths near, hearts far — in the same people, at the same service. They say the right words and keep the calendar. Nothing is missing on the outside, and nearly everything is missing on the inside. God is not describing pagans here. He is describing His own congregation.

The key word is melummadah — taught by rote, learned by drilling, recited like a schoolboy repeating a lesson he memorized but never thought about. Their fear of God, Isaiah says, is melummadah: a human commandment, learned by repetition. Reverence had been reduced to routine — the lips know it cold, and the heart has stopped reporting for duty. Rote is not evil in itself; children learn truth by repetition. But rote becomes a shelter when reciting replaces reckoning — when we can say everything about God without ever drawing near to Him.

Centuries later, Jesus picks this very verse up and lays it down again. In Matthew chapter 15, the Pharisees challenge Him because His disciples eat with unwashed hands, breaking the tradition of the elders. Jesus answers with Isaiah: "This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me; in vain do they worship me, teaching as doctrines the commandments of men." He does not retire the charge as ancient history. He reloads it. A religion of lips and traditions, kept carefully and felt not at all, was still alive in His day. We should not assume it died in ours.

But hear the turn. God's answer to rote is wonder. "Behold, I will again do wonderful things with this people, with wonder upon wonder." Wonderful is pele again — the same word we met yesterday in the farmer's field, and back in chapter 9 in the name of the child, Wonderful Counselor. Men had made God predictable, a lesson to recite. God responds by being wonderful again — beyond their reckoning, off their script — and the wisdom of their wise men perishes in the light of it. Paul quotes that line in 1 Corinthians chapter 1, at the foot of a cross no wise man saw coming.

Here is the landing for a Wednesday. God is not impressed by our fluency. He can tell the difference between lips that are near and a heart that is near, because He is looking at the second one. So before the day gets loud, come to Him with the heart first. Pray one prayer this morning that you have not memorized — halting, plain, your own words, actually meaning them. He is not asking you to perform the lesson one more time. He is asking you to draw near for real.

Lord, our lips have run ahead of our hearts; forgive our rote, draw our hearts near to You today, and do Your wonderful work in us again. Amen.

From The Ancient World

How do you move a 2,000-year-old bathtub that weighs 25 tons without breaking it? Very carefully — with steel beams, protected plaster, and a 300-ton crane.

In September, the Israel Antiquities Authority announced an unusual operation in Kiryat Gat, in southern Israel about 35 miles southwest of Jerusalem. Excavating ahead of construction in the new Carmei Gat neighborhood, archaeologists uncovered a large Jewish ritual bath — a mikveh — dating to nearly 2,000 years ago, at the end of the Second Temple period, the world of Jesus' lifetime. Rather than dismantle it, engineers reinforced its base, treated its ancient plaster, and lifted the whole plastered, stepped pool out of the ground in one piece. It measures roughly 13 feet across and weighs about 25 tons. It is held by the National Treasures Department for safekeeping, and once construction is finished, the plan is to return it and set it in a public space in the neighborhood that rose over it.

The bath matters more than the crane trick. A mikveh was a pool built for immersion under Jewish purity law — washing that restored a person to sacred life. By the Second Temple period, stepped, plastered pools like this one marked Jewish towns. Alongside the bath, the excavation found stone vessels — chalk vessels, which Jewish law regarded as resistant to ritual impurity, the same kind of stone water jars John chapter 2 describes at the wedding in Cana. Together they told the excavators something nobody knew: a Jewish settlement stood here, between the Judean lowlands and the northern Negev, where none of that period had been documented before. The directors say it was abandoned during the Bar Kokhba revolt in the second century A.D., and the ground was reoccupied some 500 years later, in the Byzantine period.

That is the quiet power of a find like this. Ordinary people took purity seriously enough to cut and plaster a pool and step down into the water, in a town history had forgotten — until a construction dig, and one very large crane, brought it back.

Source: Israel Antiquities Authority announcement, September 17, 2026, as reported by Biblical Archaeology Society, September 28, 2026.

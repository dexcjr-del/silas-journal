DEVOTIONAL

For two days now Isaiah 29 has been hard reading. Hearts far away under near lips. Counsel hidden in dark rooms. Clay talking back to the Potter. Today the chapter turns — and the turn is the point. God does not leave His people under the charge. He tells them what He is about to do with people who could not hear Him and could not see Him.

Here it is, Isaiah 29:18-19, from the ESV: "In that day the deaf shall hear the words of a book, and out of their gloom and darkness the eyes of the blind shall see. The meek shall obtain fresh joy in the LORD, and the poor among mankind shall exult in the Holy One of Israel."

"In that day." Hold those three words. Earlier in this same chapter the book was sealed — remember verses 11 and 12, the vision handed to a man who says "I cannot read it, it is sealed," and to another who says "I am not learned." The word was closed to them both. Now God says the day is coming when that same book gets heard — by deaf ears. The cure for a sealed book was never smarter men. It was opened ears, and only God opens ears.

The key word is anav — the meek, the humble, the bowed-down ones. It does not mean weak. It means people who have stopped pretending they are strong: the poor in verse 19, the ones with nothing to bring and no counsel to hide in the dark. To them God does not promise a lecture. He promises joy — and not leftover joy. "Fresh joy," joy added on top of joy, joy renewed like morning. The proud wise men of this chapter lose their wisdom. The meek get joy. That is the trade God is offering, and it has never stopped being the trade.

Jesus walked straight into this verse. In Matthew chapter 11, John the Baptist sends from prison to ask if Jesus really is the One, and Jesus answers with Isaiah's checklist: "The blind receive their sight and the lame walk, lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear." He is telling John: the day Isaiah promised is not coming someday. It is standing in front of you. And hear how the chapter itself ends, verse 24: "Those who go astray in spirit will come to understanding, and those who murmur will accept instruction." Even the murmurers. Even the wanderers. Taught at last, and glad to be taught.

Here is the landing for a Friday. You do not have to manufacture hearing. If the book has felt sealed to you — if you have read the same page three times and gotten nothing — you are exactly the reader this promise was written for. Bring God your deafness instead of hiding it. Ask Him, plainly, to open the ear and lift the gloom. The meek obtain fresh joy. Obtaining is receiving with open hands, not earning with clenched ones. Come bowed down today, and let Him do the opening.

Lord, our ears have been dull and our eyes dim; open the book to us today, and give us the fresh joy You promise the meek. Amen.

FROM THE ANCIENT WORLD

Every sacrifice, every washing, every feast in the Temple needed water — rivers of it, delivered uphill into a crowded city. This month, a long stretch of the hidden system that carried it has come back into the light.

The Israel Antiquities Authority announced on October 11 that excavations south of Kibbutz Ramat Rachel, ahead of a planned residential development, have uncovered roughly 984 feet of Jerusalem's Lower Aqueduct. Excavation directors Benyamin Storchan, Gil Freid, and Rotem Cohen date its construction to the Hasmonean kings — the Jewish rulers of the two centuries before Christ. The channel ran about 13 miles, from Solomon's Pools south of Bethlehem to the Temple Mount, and it stayed in intermittent use, incredibly, until about a century ago.

The engineering is the sermon in this story. The whole line works by gravity alone, dropping only about three feet for every mile it travels. Too steep and the water tears the channel apart; too flat and it stalls. Somebody surveyed thirteen miles of hills and valleys and held that gentle line the whole way, so that fresh, flowing water — what Scripture calls living water — arrived at the Second Temple day after day, for purification and for washing down the courts after sacrifice.

Douglas Estes, a professor of religion at New College of Florida, told Fox News Digital he is confident Jesus and His disciples used water this aqueduct supplied, though no dig can prove the use of any one person. The picture it paints needs no proving. The Temple Jesus cleansed, the courts He taught in, the feasts He came up for — all of it ran on water carried thirteen miles by a channel cut before He was born, much of it by rulers His people remembered as their own. The section uncovered will be preserved and built into the entrance of the new development, so the water line that served the Temple will greet people at the door.

Thirteen miles of stone channel, laid to a fall of three feet a mile, just so God's house would never run dry. Somebody's careful, unseen work carried the water for worship they would never live to see. That is worth remembering the next time faithful feels small.

Source: Israel Antiquities Authority announcement, October 11, 2026, as reported by the New York Post, October 16, 2026.

The Daily Read — Friday, November 6

Devotional

Yesterday we prayed Isaiah's prayer: be our arm every morning. Today, in chapter 33, we find out what the mornings looked like while Judah prayed it. They were not pretty. The envoys sent to buy peace came home weeping. The roads were empty. The land itself seemed to give up.

Isaiah 33:7-10, from the ESV: "Behold, their heroes cry aloud in the streets; the envoys of peace weep bitterly. The highways lie waste; the traveler ceases to go. Covenant is broken, pledges are despised; there is no regard for man. The land mourns and dries out; Lebanon is confounded and withers away; Sharon is like a desert, and Bashan and Carmel shake off their leaves. 'Now I will arise,' says the LORD; 'now I will lift myself up; now will I be exalted.'"

Read the list slowly, because Isaiah built it on purpose. It is a picture of everything men count on, failing in order. The heroes fail — they are crying in the streets. Diplomacy fails — the envoys of peace come back with tears instead of a treaty. The roads fail — nobody travels, because nobody is safe. Agreements fail — covenant is broken, pledges despised. Even the land fails — Sharon, famous for its flowers, turns desert; Carmel, famous for its green, shakes off its leaves like a tree in winter. When God wants us to see that a nation has run out of rope, He shows us empty highways.

Then verse 10 opens its mouth, and the whole chapter turns on three small words: Now I will arise.

The key word is qum — arise, stand up, get to your feet. It is not the word for a God who was asleep and finally woke up. God does not nap through our troubles. Qum is the word for a judge rising from his seat to deliver a verdict, or a king rising from his throne to act. The sitting was never indifference. It was patience — and patience, in Isaiah, has an appointment. David heard the same sentence from the same God in a darker hour: "Because the poor are plundered, because the needy groan, I will now arise," says the LORD; "I will place him in the safety for which he longs" (Psalm 12:5). God rises for the plundered and the groaning. He has never once risen for the proud.

Notice the timing, because it is the lesson. God did not arise while Judah still had one more envoy to send, one more payment to make, one more Egypt to call. He arose when the highways were empty — when every human road really had run out. That is not cruelty. That is mercy with its eyes open. As long as we think the next deal might save us, we will keep dealing. God loves His people too much to let a broken covenant prop them up forever.

Here is the landing for a Friday. Some of us are standing this morning in front of an empty highway — a road we counted on that simply does not go anywhere anymore. Isaiah does not tell us to pave it again. He tells us to listen. Over the sound of weeping envoys and withering hills, there is a throne room, and in it a King is standing up. Our part is not to manufacture the rescue. Our part is to be found among the poor and the groaning who waited for Him — the ones He rises for. The roads of men run out. The rising of God does not.

Lord, when our roads run out, teach us to listen for Your rising instead of running for another road; arise for us today, and set us in the safety we long for. Amen.

From The Ancient World

Most of us picture the ancient church hugging the Mediterranean — Jerusalem, Rome, Alexandria. This week the ground reminded us it also stood, enormous, on the Nile in Africa.

Archaeology News reported on November 4, 2026 that a Sudanese-Polish team at Old Dongola in Sudan has exposed a monumental staircase in the Great Church of Jesus and, at the bottom of it, reached the church's original floor for the first time. Old Dongola was the capital of Makuria, a Christian kingdom that held the middle Nile for centuries. The dig is run by the Polish Centre of Mediterranean Archaeology at the University of Warsaw with Sudan's National Corporation for Antiquities and Museums, led by Professor Artur Obłuski, whose team first encountered the building in 2021.

The scale is the headline. The church measures about 47 metres wide and 57 metres long, with walls beside the staircase still standing some 11 metres high, and the team estimates it once rose to about 21 metres. Obłuski compares its role in Nubia to Hagia Sophia's in Constantinople — a trendsetter — and notes it is the largest church known from medieval Nubia. Only a small part has been opened so far; the staircase is the one place the floor has been reached, and scholars must now rethink a church outside the city walls that was long taken for Dongola's cathedral.

A smaller surprise sat above it: a little church on the upper floor of a structure added to the cathedral, its walls only a metre high, left painted plaster and fragments of Greek inscriptions. Epigraphist Dr. Agata Deptuła identified them as biblical odes — the prayer-songs attached to the Psalter — including the Prayer of Hannah, written in a careful hand with letters about half a centimetre tall. Psalm texts were the common biblical writing at Dongola before; the odes are new for the site.

A cathedral bigger than most of Europe's parish churches, in a kingdom many believers have never heard of, with Hannah's prayer copied out in tiny Greek letters. The faith was never as small, or as northern, as our maps make it feel.

Source: Archaeology News Online Magazine, November 4, 2026, reporting the Polish Centre of Mediterranean Archaeology (University of Warsaw) and Sudan's National Corporation for Antiquities and Museums, Old Dongola excavations led by Prof. Artur Obłuski.

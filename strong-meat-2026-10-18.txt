DEVOTIONAL

Yesterday God annulled Jerusalem's covenant with death and set His plumb line against their wall. Today He shows us what life inside that false shelter actually feels like — and He does it with the plainest picture in the whole chapter: a bed that does not fit the man trying to sleep in it.

Here is the passage, Isaiah 28:19-20, from the ESV: "As often as it passes through it will take you; for morning by morning it will pass through, by day and by night; and it will be sheer terror to understand the message. For the bed is too short to stretch oneself on, and the covering too narrow to wrap oneself in."

Picture it. A tired man climbs into bed, stretches out — and his feet hang off the end. He pulls the blanket up — and it will not cover him. He curls, he twists. There is no rest in that bed, not because he is not tired, but because the bed is too small for the need he brought to it. That, God says, is your covenant with death: a shelter built to sleep through the storm in, and it cannot hold you. Morning by morning, instead of rest, the flood passes through and takes you again.

The key word is qatsar, the Hebrew word for short — too short, cut down, not enough. It is the same word Moses heard on God's own lips in the wilderness, when God asked, "Is the LORD's hand shortened?" The answer there was no, and the answer here is just as clear — only the direction is reversed. God's hand was never the short thing. Our beds are. Every refuge we make for ourselves comes up short: too short to stretch out on, too narrow to wrap up in. We keep measuring our shelters by how they feel at noon. Isaiah measures them at 2 a.m., when the terror passes through and a man finds out what his covering really covers.

Now hear David, who slept in a very different bed. In Psalm 4 he writes, "In peace I will both lie down and sleep; for you alone, O LORD, make me dwell in safety." Same nighttime. Same lying down. Entirely different rest. David was not a man without enemies. But his bed was not a deal he had cut; his covering was a God who does not come up short. Put the two pictures side by side and the chapter comes into focus: Jerusalem refused God's rest back in verse 12, so they got the rest they built instead — a bed too short, terror by night.

Here is the landing for a Sunday morning. We all have a bed we try to sleep in — the plan that will finally make us safe, the number that will finally let us breathe, the distraction that finally quiets the fear. Isaiah is not mocking tired people. He is telling them the truth before the night gets long: it is too short, friend. It will not hold you, and some part of you already knows it. But the God whose hand is not shortened still offers the rest they refused — and in Christ He offers it as a person, not a bargain. You do not need a bigger bed of your own making. You need to lie down, today, in the safety only He gives, and let Him be the covering that is finally enough.

Lord, our beds are too short and our coverings too narrow; teach us to stop curling up in shelters we made, and to lie down and sleep in the safety of Christ alone. Amen.

FROM THE ANCIENT WORLD

Most church history in Italy gets told from Rome. This week, a quieter city got its chapter back.

On October 15, Ca' Foscari University of Venice announced that excavations at the Altino Archaeological Park, in 2025 and 2026, have located what researchers believe is the long-lost episcopal church of Altinum, the Roman city on the mainland shore of the Venetian Lagoon. The church measures about 30 by 20 meters — roughly 98 by 66 feet — and faces east rather than following Altinum's Roman street grid, one of the marks of an early Christian place of worship. The evidence dates it to between the late fourth and the first half of the fifth century. That dating is tantalizing, because Saint Jerome mentioned a church at Altinum in A.D. 396, describing the care given to its altar and sacred vessels by the circle of Bishop Heliodorus. The team is careful, and so should we be: this may be Jerome's church, but that is not confirmed.

Beside it stands a second substantial building, possibly two stories, which the excavators suggest may have been the bishop's residence. Its foundations hold a sermon in stone: large paving blocks taken up from Roman roads and laid upside down as building material. Roman roads were public works, protected by law — a bishop who could quarry them to build his house was a man whose authority in his city had become very real.

In the sixth and seventh centuries a portico and a cemetery of brick-lined burials grew up in front of the church. Then the story turns: the complex was systematically dismantled, its stone carried off, as Altinum's episcopal center moved to Torcello, out in the lagoon — a transfer consistent with a Torcello inscription dated A.D. 639. Excavation director Diego Calaon reads it not as a panicked flight from invaders, the way the old story of Venice's founding is often told, but as a long, organized transformation. The old city did not vanish. It became the new one, stone by stone.

Why does it matter to us? Because this is what the faith actually looked like on the ground sixteen centuries ago — not just creeds and councils, but a bishop, a church facing the sunrise, burials gathered close to the worship, and a congregation that, when its world shifted, carried the faith with it into the water. Next, the team hopes to find the missing baptistery.

Source: Ca' Foscari University of Venice announcement, October 15, 2026, reporting the Altinum excavations directed by Diego Calaon.

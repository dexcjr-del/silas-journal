DEVOTIONAL

Yesterday we watched God lay a cornerstone in Zion while everybody else in Jerusalem was running scared. A foundation is good news. But it raises a question nobody in that city wanted to ask: what about the rest of the building? Jerusalem had been building too — alliances, shelters, a whole life on a deal they thought would keep them safe. Today God walks onto their construction site with a measuring line in His hand.

Here is the passage, Isaiah 28:17-18, from the ESV: "And I will make justice the line, and righteousness the plumb line; and hail will sweep away the refuge of lies, and waters will overwhelm the shelter. Then your covenant with death will be annulled, and your agreement with Sheol will not stand."

Go back to what these leaders bragged about in verse 15: "We have made a covenant with death, and with Sheol we have an agreement." Sit with how strange that is. These were God's own people, and their safety plan was a deal cut with death itself — treaties with Egypt, politics in the dark, anything to keep the storm from reaching their door. They thought they had negotiated with the grave and won. God says the contract is void. Not because He is cruel. Because death never had the authority to sign it.

The key word is mishpat, the Hebrew word for justice. It is not just courtroom language. Mishpat is things set right — the world measured and ordered the way God intended it. And look at His tools: a line and a plumb line. A plumb line is just a weight on a string, but it tells the truth no builder can argue with. Hang it beside a wall and it shows, without opinion, whether the wall stands straight or leans. God is saying: I am not measuring your life by your neighbors, or by your intentions, or by the deal you cut. I am measuring it by what is actually straight.

Amos saw the same picture generations later. In Amos chapter 7, the Lord stands beside a wall with a plumb line in His hand and says, "Behold, I am setting a plumb line in the midst of my people Israel." Same tool. Same God. He does not measure to embarrass us. He measures because a leaning wall eventually falls on the people living inside it, and He would rather show us the lean now than let the house come down later.

Here is the landing, and it connects straight back to yesterday's stone. Jerusalem's leaders had two things: a covenant with death that could not stand, and a cornerstone God had laid that could not fall. Every one of us builds on one of those. We make our little agreements with death all the time: the savings that will keep us safe, the reputation that will keep us respected, the distraction that keeps us from thinking about the grave. Isaiah says those contracts get annulled. The hail comes. The waters rise. What survives the measuring is only what was built on the tested stone. So the question this morning is not whether God is measuring. It is whether what you are building lines up with the Stone He laid — the only wall in this chapter still standing at the end.

Lord, measure us by Your line and not our own; tear down every refuge of lies we have trusted, and build our lives on Christ, Your tested cornerstone. Amen.

FROM THE ANCIENT WORLD

Sometimes archaeology starts with an expedition. This week it started with a man working the ground in front of his own house.

On October 13, Egypt's Supreme Council of Antiquities announced that a resident of Behbeit El-Hagar, a village in Egypt's Nile Delta, had found an inscribed stone block while working land in front of his home — and did the right thing. He stopped and reported it. Antiquities inspectors came, and the rescue excavation that followed uncovered the remains of two colossal royal statues identified with Ramesses II, the great pharaoh of Egypt's 19th Dynasty, who ruled roughly 1279 to 1213 B.C.

The first find is a head, about 1.6 meters tall — over five feet, for just the head. The king wears the double crown of Upper and Lower Egypt, and part of the back pillar survives, carrying his Horus name, "Hor Ka Nakht Mery Maat." From that head, archaeologists estimate the finished statue stood about 8.5 meters — nearly 28 feet, taller than a two-story house. Nearby lay a second royal statue, a section about 2.4 meters long, legs and base missing but its face under the striped nemes headdress well preserved; the team believes it also depicts Ramesses. A third piece, a pink granite block, carries his royal titles: "King of Upper and Lower Egypt" and "Son of Ra." The village was already known for a major temple of Isis; what these older royal pieces were doing there is the open question, and the dig continues.

Why do a pair of broken statues matter to us? Because Ramesses the Great is the name most often attached — by tradition, not by proof — to the pharaoh of the Exodus. Let me be careful, the way we should always be careful: Exodus never names its pharaoh, and scholars argue about it to this day. But Exodus chapter 1 does tell us the Israelites built a store city called Raamses, and a king who covered Egypt with monuments to himself is exactly the kind who would build with other people's backs. These stones do not prove the Exodus. They give us its world — the scale of the power Israel faced, and the size of the God who brought them out anyway. Three thousand years later, the king's face comes up out of a farmer's field, his empire in pieces. The people he oppressed are still reading their Book this morning.

Source: Egypt's Supreme Council of Antiquities announcement, October 13, 2026, as reported by Ahram Online and the Egyptian Gazette.

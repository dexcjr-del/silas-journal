DEVOTIONAL

All week we have been in Isaiah 28, and it has not been a cheerful room. Drunk priests mocking the prophet. God's plain teaching turned down. An offer of rest refused by people too busy running to receive it. If the chapter ended there, it would just be a sad story about stubborn men. But God does not end the chapter there. After the refusal, He does something nobody in that room expected: He starts building.

Here is the verse, Isaiah 28:16, from the ESV: "Therefore thus says the Lord GOD, 'Behold, I am the one who has laid as a foundation in Zion, a stone, a tested stone, a precious cornerstone, of a sure foundation: "Whoever believes will not be in haste."'"

Sit with the scene. Jerusalem is scrambling — messengers to Egypt, deals in the dark, kings trying to outrun Assyria with politics. Everybody is in a hurry because everybody is afraid. And into the middle of that panic, God does not shout. He lays a stone. Slow, deliberate, foundation work. Men build treaties when they are scared. God builds foundations.

The key word is the Hebrew word pinnah, the cornerstone. In the ancient world this was not decoration. The cornerstone was the first stone set, the one squared and tested, and every other stone in the building took its line from it. If the cornerstone was true, the walls could be true. If it was off, the whole house leaned, no matter how hard the builders worked. God says His stone is tested — proven before it is trusted — and precious. And notice Who does the laying. "I am the one who has laid" it. Not Israel. Not the king. God Himself set the foundation, because the one thing a panicking people cannot do is save themselves straight.

Now follow the stone through Scripture, because the apostles could not leave this verse alone. Peter quotes it outright in 1 Peter chapter 2: "Behold, I am laying in Zion a stone, a cornerstone chosen and precious, and whoever believes in him will not be put to shame." Then Peter adds the hard part — the same stone that is a foundation to the one who believes becomes a stone of stumbling to the one who will not. Same stone. Two outcomes. The difference is not the stone. It is whether a man builds on it or trips over it. Paul says the same in Romans 9. Isaiah's cornerstone turned out to have a name, and His name is Jesus. The foundation Jerusalem needed, God laid in Zion — and then, centuries later, He laid it in a manger, on a cross, and out of an empty tomb.

Here is the landing, and it is aimed right at the hurry in us: "Whoever believes will not be in haste." Not "will not have trouble." Will not be in haste — will not panic, will not scramble, will not run to Egypt every time the news turns bad. Yesterday we saw that God's people refused His rest. Today we see why the rest was safe to take: because underneath it sits a foundation God laid Himself, tested and true. You do not have to hold the building up today. You have to stand on the stone that is already laid.

Lord, when we are tempted to hurry and scheme, settle us on Your tested stone; teach us to believe, and in believing, to be still. Amen.

FROM THE ANCIENT WORLD

Yesterday we talked about the aqueduct that carried water to the Temple. This week, archaeologists announced what that water may have fed — and it is a remarkable find.

On October 13, The Jewish Weekly reported that a Hebrew University excavation team, led by Professors Uzi Leibner and Orit Peleg-Barkat, has uncovered a monumental purification complex in the Ophel, the ground immediately south of the Temple Mount in Jerusalem, near one of its main gates. Dating to the late Second Temple period — the first century before Christ through A.D. 70 — the complex covers roughly 280 square meters and includes three large ritual baths, a big plastered pool about 12 by 9.5 meters with steps descending on all four sides, a paved plaza, and an extensive water system. One bath survives to its full height of about 4.2 meters, vaulted ceiling and all — a floor-to-ceiling survival the excavators call exceedingly rare in Jerusalem. They describe a structure devoted entirely to ritual purification, sitting at the foot of the Temple Mount, as an unparalleled phenomenon.

Underneath it, the team found an even earlier purification complex, probably from the first century B.C. — meaning pilgrims were washing at this spot, generation after generation, before going up to worship. And the end of the story is written in the floors: dozens of coins from the First Revolt against Rome were found in the destruction debris. The complex died in the summer of A.D. 70, when Rome destroyed the city and the Temple, and it has been silent ever since.

Why does this matter to us? Because it shows us, in stone, something the New Testament assumes we know. When Luke tells us crowds came up to Jerusalem for the feasts, and when Acts describes thousands baptized at Pentecost near the Temple, this is the world underneath the words — real pilgrims, real water, a real longing to be clean before drawing near to God. The pipes and pools have changed. The longing has not. And Christians confess that the cleansing those baths could only picture, Christ accomplished once for all.

Source: The Jewish Weekly, October 13, 2026, reporting the Hebrew University of Jerusalem Ophel excavations.

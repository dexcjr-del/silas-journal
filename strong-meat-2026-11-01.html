The Daily Read — Sunday, November 1

Strong Meat — The Daily Read · November 1, 2026 · by Silas · 964 words · plain text

Devotional

Yesterday the Lion growled over His prey and the Bird hovered over the nest, and God would not be shouted down. Today Isaiah closes the chapter, and he closes it with a single word aimed at Judah's feet: turn.

Isaiah 31:6-9, from the ESV: "Turn to him from whom people have deeply revolted, O children of Israel. For in that day everyone shall cast away his idols of silver and his idols of gold, which your hands have sinfully made for you. And the Assyrian shall fall by a sword, not of man; and a sword, not of man, shall devour him; and he shall flee from the sword, and his young men shall be put to forced labor. His rock shall pass away in terror, and his officers desert the standard in panic, declares the LORD, whose fire is in Zion and whose furnace is in Jerusalem."

Notice where the chapter lands. It opened with a woe on people going down to Egypt. It ends with an open door. God does not say, "Clean yourselves up and then come." He says turn — and the casting away of the idols happens "in that day," as they turn, not before it. The silver and gold things their own hands made get thrown down on the road home, not at some inspection gate before the road is allowed.

The key word is shuv — turn, return. It is the Bible's great turning word, and it is a word about feet before it is a word about feelings. Shuv is what a man does on a road: he stops, he turns around, he walks the other way. Hosea would preach it to the northern kingdom in the same word, in Hosea 14:1: "Return, O Israel, to the LORD your God, for you have stumbled because of your iniquity." Joel used it too: "return to me with all your heart." God is not asking Judah for a mood. He is asking for a direction. Sorry is good. Shuv is sorrow with shoes on.

Then the promise we were left holding yesterday gets paid. Assyria "shall fall by a sword, not of man" — and in Isaiah 37, Jerusalem woke to exactly that: the camp struck in the night, and Sennacherib went home to Nineveh, where his own sons cut him down in the temple of his god. His "rock" passed away in terror, just as verse 9 says. Assyria had a rock, and it crumbled. Zion has a furnace — God's own fire, burning at home, in His city, for His people. The enemy's strength evaporates. God's fire stays lit.

Here is the landing for a Sunday. You do not have to finish the Egypt road just because you started down it. The chapter that began with a woe ends with an invitation, and the invitation is one word long. Whatever direction your feet have been pointed this week — toward the horses, toward the strong people, toward the thing your hands made and then started trusting — today is a good day to shuv. Turn first. Throw the idols down as you come. The Lion has not moved, the Bird is still hovering, and the fire in Zion is still burning for the people who turn toward it.

Lord, we have revolted and run our own road; turn our feet home to You today, and we will cast down what our hands made as we come. Amen.

From The Ancient World

Kings wore purple because purple cost a fortune — and this fall, researchers reading dirt under a microscope found the factory floor where Israel made it, and it turns out the place kept changing jobs with the seasons.

The site is Tel Shiqmona, on the coast at the foot of Mount Carmel, south of modern Haifa. It holds the largest known Iron Age purple-dye factory in the Mediterranean — a state-run asset of the Kingdom of Israel. The dye came from murex sea snails, crushed by the thousand for a little unfading color — the color of the tabernacle curtains in Exodus 26 and of the worthy woman's clothing in Proverbs 31:22. A new peer-reviewed study in PLOS One, from a University of Haifa team led by Ivgenia Ogloblin Ramirez and Golan Shalvi, re-examined the layer dated about 790 to 740 B.C. — the days of Jeroboam II, when Amos and Hosea were preaching — in the floors of the 2023 excavation.

The dye evidence is plain: more than forty dye-production artifacts, over half of them stained purple, and at least sixteen vats of roughly 350 liters each. The surprise is everything else in the same rooms. Olive pits and a press say olives were crushed there in late autumn. Microscopic dung pellets say livestock sheltered indoors through winter. Loom weights say textiles were worked in spring. Between seasons, workers spread wood ash to neutralize the floors and reset the rooms. One industrial compound, four trades, run on the calendar like a farm.

Hold the honest line: the seasonal rotation is the researchers' reconstruction from those floor traces, and they say plainly that the dye-making's own season is still uncertain. But the picture rings true to the Bible's world. Purple did not come from palaces. It came from working people on Carmel's shore, grinding beauty out of shellfish in rooms that smelled of olives and animals by turns — and centuries later a woman named Lydia would make her living selling that same purple in Philippi, and hear Paul preach. The color of kings was always somebody's day's work.

Source: Ogloblin Ramirez, Shalvi, Pinsky, et al., "Seasonal and multi-functional use of industrial space at Iron Age Tel Shiqmona, Israel," PLOS One, September 24, 2026; University of Haifa research announcement, October 5, 2026.

DEVOTIONAL

Yesterday God offered His running people rest — "In returning and rest you shall be saved" — and they answered, "No! We will flee upon horses." Isaiah told them how that race would end: a thousand fleeing at the threat of one, left like a flagstaff on a hill. So read the first word of today's verse slowly. It makes no sense unless grace is real. Therefore.

Isaiah 30:18-21, from the ESV: "Therefore the LORD waits to be gracious to you, and therefore he exalts himself to show mercy to you. For the LORD is a God of justice; blessed are all those who wait for him. For a people shall dwell in Zion, in Jerusalem; you shall weep no more. He will surely be gracious to you at the sound of your cry. As soon as he hears it, he answers you. And though the Lord give you the bread of adversity and the water of affliction, yet your Teacher will not hide himself anymore, but your eyes shall see your Teacher. And your ears shall hear a word behind you, saying, 'This is the way, walk in it,' when you turn to the right or when you turn to the left."

Did you catch it? Rebellion is the reason given for mercy. Not in spite of the horses — therefore. God looked at a people sprinting toward Egypt and decided the next move was His: waiting to be gracious.

The key word is chakah — to wait, to long for. It appears on both sides of this verse, and that is the whole sermon. God chakahs for His people, and blessed are the people who chakah for Him. Two waitings, facing each other. His is not the waiting of a man unsure what to do. It is a Father's waiting — He knows the running has to run itself out, and He means to be there, gracious, the moment His child cries out. Verse 19 says the instant He hears that cry, He answers. His waiting was never distance. It was readiness.

Notice what He does not promise. He does not promise to remove the bread of adversity and the water of affliction right away. Some Mondays start with that bread already on the table. What He promises is better than an escape: "Your Teacher will not hide himself anymore." Your eyes see Him. Your ears hear Him — a word behind you, close enough to follow, when you cannot tell right from left anymore.

David prayed from the same place in Psalm 27:14: "Wait for the LORD; be strong, and let your heart take courage; wait for the LORD!" Waiting on God is not doing nothing. It is refusing to do the Egypt thing — strength with its boots off, listening for the voice behind.

Here is the landing for a Monday. You will be tempted this week to outrun what you should out-wait — to force the door, buy the alliance, anything but sit still under a God who seems slow. Hear Isaiah: God is not slow. He is waiting to be gracious, and He calls the people who wait with Him blessed. Cry out first. Then stand where He put you, eyes on the Teacher, ears open. This is the way. You do not have to invent it. Walk in it.

Lord, we have run ahead of You and called it faithfulness; teach us to wait as You wait, and let us hear Your voice behind us today. Amen.

FROM THE ANCIENT WORLD

Before a pilgrim in Jesus' day could climb up to the Temple, he first went down — down steps, into water, and back up clean. This fall, archaeologists finished uncovering how seriously Jerusalem built for that going-down.

The Hebrew University of Jerusalem announced on September 24 a monumental purification complex in the Ophel, immediately south of the Temple Mount, near the Triple Gate, one of its main entrances. After five seasons of excavation — the last concluding this past summer — directors Uzi Leibner and Orit Peleg-Barkat uncovered a complex built in the early first century, in the lifetime of Jesus. At its center sits a plastered pool, about 39 by 31 feet, with steps descending from all four sides, large enough for dozens at once. Beside it stands a stone building of four rooms, three of them large public ritual baths — mikvaot, in Hebrew. Eighteen more baths from earlier digs surround it.

One bath is the wonder of the place. It survives from floor to ceiling, all 13.8 feet of it, its stone vault and water shaft still intact — a preservation the university calls exceedingly rare for Second Temple Jerusalem. Underneath runs a drainage system with channels tall enough in places to walk through, carrying water toward the Kidron Valley. On the floors lay coins from the First Jewish Revolt, the war that ended when Rome destroyed the city and Temple in A.D. 70 and buried this complex with them.

The excavators call a building devoted entirely to purification, set at a Temple gate, "a truly unparalleled phenomenon." Feast days poured crowds into that city, and every worshiper needed washing before worship. Somebody planned pools and flowing water for people they would never meet, so strangers could go up to God's house clean. It also throws quiet light on Acts chapter 2: when three thousand were baptized in Jerusalem in one day at Pentecost, the city already had the water, and the place, to do it.

Source: Hebrew University of Jerusalem announcement, September 24, 2026, republished by American Friends of the Hebrew University, October 2, 2026; reported by Patterns of Evidence, October 23, 2026.

The Daily Read — Wednesday, November 11

Devotional

Yesterday the desert bloomed and we were told to strengthen weak hands and speak to hurried hearts. Today Isaiah stays in that same desert and tells us what happens when God Himself arrives. The blossoms were only the beginning. Now the people themselves start to change.

Isaiah 35:5-10, from the ESV: "Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf unstopped; then shall the lame man leap like a deer, and the tongue of the mute sing for joy. For waters break forth in the wilderness, and streams in the desert; the burning sand shall become a pool, the thirsty ground springs of water; in the haunt of jackals, where they lie down, the grass shall become reeds and rushes. And a highway shall be there, and it shall be called the Way of Holiness; the unclean shall not pass over it. It shall belong to those who walk on the way; even if they are fools, they shall not go astray. No lion shall be there, nor shall any ravenous beast come up on it; they shall not be found there, but the redeemed shall walk there. And the ransomed of the LORD shall return and come to Zion with singing; everlasting joy shall be upon their heads; they shall obtain gladness and joy, and sorrow and sighing shall flee away."

Notice the order. Eyes first, then ears, then legs, then tongue. The lame man does not just walk — he leaps like a deer. The mute do not just speak — they sing for joy. Isaiah never does halfway healings. And the land heals alongside the people: burning sand becomes a pool, and the place where jackals bedded down grows reeds and rushes, plants that only live where water stays.

Then a road appears in that watered desert — a highway called the Way of Holiness. It is a safe road, and Isaiah is plain about why. No lion. No ravenous beast. And the travelers do not have to be clever to stay on it: even fools shall not go astray on this road. The safety is not in the walkers. It is in the way God built the road.

The key word comes twice at the end, in two forms: the redeemed, and the ransomed of the LORD. Behind them stand two Hebrew words for buying somebody back — ga'al, the kinsman who steps in because you belong to his family, and padah, the one who pays the price to set a captive free. Isaiah piles them together on purpose. The people on this highway are there for both reasons: they are family, and a price was paid. Nobody on that road wandered there. They were brought back.

Centuries later, John the Baptist sat in prison and sent messengers to ask whether Jesus really was the One. Jesus answered by quoting this chapter: "Go and tell John what you hear and see: the blind receive their sight and the lame walk, lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear, and the dead are raised up, and the poor have good news preached to them" (Matthew 11:4-5). He did not argue. He pointed at Isaiah 35 walking around in front of people. The healings were the sign that the highway was being built.

Here is the landing for a Wednesday. Some of us worry we are too foolish to stay on the road — too prone to wander, too slow to learn. Isaiah already thought of us. The Way of Holiness was made for ransomed people, not impressive ones, and even fools do not lose it. Stay on the road God made, keep walking toward Zion, and let the One who bought you back handle the lions. Sorrow and sighing are not at the end of this story. They flee away. Singing is at the end of it.

Lord, open our eyes and unstop our ears today; put us on Your holy way, keep us from straying, and teach our tongues to sing for joy. Amen.

From The Ancient World

Down the Nile in Sudan, archaeologists have just stood on a church floor nobody had reached before — in a Christian kingdom many of us were never taught existed.

The Polish Centre of Mediterranean Archaeology at the University of Warsaw announced on November 4, 2026 that its joint Sudanese-Polish expedition at Old Dongola, led by Professor Artur Obłuski with Sudan's National Corporation for Antiquities and Museums, has uncovered a monumental staircase in the Great Church of Jesus and reached the cathedral's original floor level for the first time. Old Dongola was the capital of Makuria, a Christian kingdom on the Nile, and this church is the largest known from medieval Nubia: about 47 meters wide and 57 meters long, cautiously estimated at some 21 meters high, with walls beside the staircase still standing about 11 meters. The team identified the building in 2021; only a small part has been excavated so far.

The surprises kept coming above ground. In a structure added onto the cathedral, the team found a second, smaller church on an upper floor. Its walls survive only about a meter high, but its fill held fragments of painted plaster — six large shelving units of it — and small fragments bearing Greek inscriptions. The expedition's epigraphist, Dr. Agata Deptuła, identified the texts as biblical odes, the songs of prayer appended to the Psalter. One fragment is part of the Prayer of Hannah, the mother of Samuel, written in a careful hand with letters only about half a centimeter tall. Psalm texts were the common finds at Old Dongola before; the odes are new for the site.

Sit with that picture a moment. On the Nile, far south of Egypt, a great African church sang Hannah's song in Greek, on a scale larger than many European cathedrals of its day. Christianity in Africa is not a late import, and it was never small. A people Isaiah's world would have called distant were keeping the songs of Scripture in a house built for Jesus. The redeemed, it turns out, have been walking home from further away, and for longer, than we tend to remember — and now we can stand on their floor.

Source: Polish Centre of Mediterranean Archaeology, University of Warsaw, announcement November 4, 2026, reported November 10, 2026; joint expedition with Sudan's National Corporation for Antiquities and Museums at Old Dongola, Sudan.

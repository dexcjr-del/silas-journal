The Daily Read — Friday, October 30

Strong Meat — The Daily Read · October 30, 2026 · by Silas · 970 words · plain text

Devotional

Yesterday the rod fell to music, and the burning place turned out to be prepared for the king, not for God's people. Today Isaiah opens a new chapter, and he opens it by following Judah's messengers south — down the road to Egypt, going back for help to the very house they once escaped.

Isaiah 31:1-3, from the ESV: "Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help and rely on horses, who trust in chariots because they are many and in horsemen because they are very strong, but do not look to the Holy One of Israel or consult the LORD! And yet he is wise and brings disaster; he does not call back his words, but will arise against the house of the evildoers and against the helpers of those who work iniquity. The Egyptians are man, and not God, and their horses are flesh, and not spirit. When the LORD stretches out his hand, the helper will stumble, and he who is helped will fall, and they will all perish together."

Hear the direction in that first line. They "go down" to Egypt. In Scripture you always go down to Egypt and up to Jerusalem. Down is not just geography here. It is a man trading the high place for the low one, trading trust for a purchase. Egypt had horses to sell — many chariots, strong horsemen, numbers a frightened king could count. And counting felt safer than praying.

The key word is basar — flesh. Verse 3 sets it in a pair: Egypt's horses are flesh, "and not spirit." The Hebrew word for spirit is ruach, the same word for breath and wind — the breath of God that hovered over the waters at the beginning. Flesh is real, and flesh is strong, but flesh tires, flesh panics, flesh dies. Isaiah is not saying Egypt is weak. He is saying Egypt is the wrong kind of strong. A horse can carry you fast. It cannot carry you finally.

And notice what Judah did not do. They did not look to the Holy One of Israel. They did not consult the LORD. The sin here is not mainly that they bought horses. It is that they never asked. They made the whole plan — messengers, money, treaty — without one honest prayer in it. God was not rejected in anger. He was simply left out, the way we leave Him out when the phone call feels more practical than prayer.

Jeremiah would say it plainer a century later, in Jeremiah 17:5: "Cursed is the man who trusts in man and makes flesh his strength, whose heart turns away from the LORD." Same word — flesh — same turn of the heart. And Isaiah tells us how the Egypt plan ends: the helper stumbles, the helped falls, "and they will all perish together." Lean on flesh, and you do not just fall. You fall in company, both of you in the same heap.

Here is the landing for a Friday. Before you go down, look up. Whatever your Egypt is — the loan, the alliance, the strong person whose favor feels like safety — ask one question first: did I consult the LORD about this, or did I just count the horses? God is not against help. He is against help that replaces Him. He is wise, Isaiah says, and He does not call back His words. So put the consultation first, before the messengers ride. Flesh will do what flesh does. The Spirit does not stumble, and neither does the man who leans on Him.

Lord, forgive us for counting horses before we ever asked You; teach us to look to You first, and to lean on Spirit and not on flesh. Amen.

From The Ancient World

Before a pilgrim could climb to the Temple, he had to go down into the water. This month, archaeologists in Jerusalem uncovered the washing place built for the crowds.

The site is the Ophel, the ground immediately south of the Temple Mount, near the Triple Gate, one of the main entrances up to the Temple. After five seasons of excavation, a Hebrew University team directed by Prof. Uzi Leibner and Prof. Orit Peleg-Barkat has uncovered a monumental purification complex from the late Second Temple period, announced October 13. It covers about 280 square meters and holds three large ritual baths — mikva'ot — a paved plaza, an extensive water system, and, at its center, a large plastered pool built in the early first century, about 12 by 9.5 meters, with steps descending into it from all four sides.

One bath survives to its full height of 4.2 meters, vaulted stone ceiling and all — a rare thing in Jerusalem, where Second Temple buildings usually come up as foundations. Drainage channels more than two meters tall carried water toward the Kidron Valley, and the team thinks the complex may have been fed by a branch of the aqueduct that supplied the Temple. Underneath sits an earlier purification complex from the first century B.C., so pilgrims were washing on this spot long before this grand version was built.

The end is written in the floors. Dozens of coins from the First Revolt against Rome were found in the destruction debris — the complex died in the summer of A.D. 70, with the Temple itself. Until that summer, this was the last stop before worship for crowds who had walked days to get there: down the steps, under the water, up the hill, clean. The Psalms of Ascent were sung by people whose feet had stood in rooms like these.

Source: Hebrew University of Jerusalem, Institute of Archaeology excavation announcement, October 13, 2026, reported by The Jewish Weekly, October 13, 2026, and Exploring Bible Lands, October 26, 2026.

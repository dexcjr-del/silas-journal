DEVOTIONAL

Yesterday the Name came from afar, and God's people sang on the feast night while He did the fighting. Today Isaiah closes the chapter, and he closes it with a sound I did not expect: music, keeping time with the blows.

Isaiah 30:31-33, from the ESV: "The Assyrians will be terror-stricken at the voice of the LORD, when he strikes with his rod. And every stroke of the appointed staff that the LORD lays on them will be to the sound of tambourines and lyres. Battling with brandished arm, he will fight with them. For a burning place has long been prepared; indeed, for the king it is made ready, its pyre made deep and wide, with fire and wood in abundance; the breath of the LORD, like a stream of sulfur, kindles it."

Start with the rod, because Isaiah started there twenty chapters ago. In Isaiah 10:5, God called Assyria itself "the rod of my anger; the staff in their hands is my fury." Assyria was God's instrument, raised up to discipline His own people. But the instrument got proud, and God answered in Isaiah 10:15: "Shall the axe boast over him who hews with it, or the saw magnify itself against him who wields it? As if a rod should wield him who lifts it!" Now the sentence lands. The rod gets the rod. The empire that thought it held the staff finds out whose hand was on it all along.

The key word is shevet — rod. It is a ruler's staff, not a word for wild violence. A sword slaughters. A rod measures. It lands stroke by stroke, in a hand, on purpose. That is why verse 32 calls it "the appointed staff." Appointed. Every stroke was counted before it fell. God does not swing wild at empires, and He does not swing wild at you. What He appoints has a number on it, a hand over it, and an end to it.

Then hear the strangest part. Every stroke falls "to the sound of tambourines and lyres." While God fights, His people make music — yesterday's feast-night song, still going. Judah does not lift the staff. Judah lifts the lyre. The battle has a soundtrack, and it is not panic. It is praise, timed to a fight God is winning without their help.

And the chapter ends at a pyre. The burning place — Topheth — "has long been prepared." Not improvised in anger. Long prepared, deep and wide, made ready not for the people who cried out in verse 19, but "for the king" who came against them. God was ready for Assyria before Assyria was ready for Judah.

Here is the landing for a Thursday. Do not mistake the instrument for the master. The pressure on your life — the hard circumstance God is allowing, the strong thing standing over you — may feel like it owns the staff. It does not. A rod is only ever in a hand, the hand is His, and the strokes are appointed, not endless. And do not fear the burning place. It was not prepared for the child who cries out. It was prepared for the pride that refuses to bow. Your part is Judah's part: keep the music going, and let the hand that holds the rod finish its work.

Lord, teach us to hear the lyre over the battle, and to trust that every stroke we feel is appointed by Your hand and none of them is wasted. Amen.

FROM THE ANCIENT WORLD

A treasurer went into his grave with a doctor's tools, and nobody can yet explain why. Egypt's antiquities ministry announced the puzzle this week.

At Saqqara, the great burial ground south of Cairo, an Egyptian mission from the Supreme Council of Antiquities has uncovered a mudbrick mastaba — a bench-shaped tomb — northwest of the pyramid of King Merenre, a Sixth Dynasty pharaoh who ruled around 2280 B.C. One shaft leads down to the inscribed burial chamber of an official named Weni. His walls, painted with rows of offerings, still carry his name and titles: mayor, sem priest, treasurer of the king of Lower Egypt, sole companion, lord of the city of El-Kab. A senior court man, close to the king. Minister Sherif Fathy visited on October 26 and descended the shaft himself.

In that shaft lay bronze instruments officials believe were likely used for medicine — a rare find in a burial, according to Mohamed Abdel Badie, head of the Egyptian Antiquities Sector. Here is the honest part: nothing in Weni's inscriptions calls him a physician. The tools may have treated the man himself. They may be ritual kit — a sem priest performed the funerary ceremonies — or something else. Further study is planned, and until it lands, the medical reading is a good question, not a settled answer.

The rest of the tomb is just as human. In a vaulted room above the chamber stood two small wooden statues in a rare devotional posture. A false door names a woman, Sekhet-neferti, a priestess of Hathor. And Weni's skeleton tells a plainer story than his titles: he died past fifty, with advanced disease of his teeth and gums, and bones marked by years of physical strain. He is not the famous Weni the Elder of Abydos — the ministry presents a separate official; the name was common. This is Egypt centuries before Joseph, an ordered, literate world already old when Abraham was born — and it still has names left to give up.

Source: Egyptian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities / Supreme Council of Antiquities statement, reported by the Egyptian Gazette and SEE News, October 26, 2026; Arkeonews, October 27, 2026.

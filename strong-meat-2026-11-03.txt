DEVOTIONAL

Yesterday the King came into view — a king reigning in righteousness, his people standing on noble things. Today Isaiah turns from the King to the comfortable, and the room gets quiet. Chapter 32 is not done with us yet.

Isaiah 32:9, 15-18, from the ESV: "Rise up, you women who are at ease, hear my voice; you complacent daughters, give ear to my speech... until the Spirit is poured upon us from on high, and the wilderness becomes a fruitful field, and the fruitful field is deemed a forest. Then justice will dwell in the wilderness, and righteousness abide in the fruitful field. And the effect of righteousness will be peace, and the result of righteousness, quietness and trust forever. My people will abide in a peaceful habitation, in secure dwellings, and in quiet resting places."

Start with the women at ease, because Isaiah does. These are not villains. They are comfortable people in a comfortable season, and the prophet tells them to rise up and listen, because within a year the vintage will fail and the carefree city will shake. Ease, in Isaiah, is not rest. It is sleep with the doors unlocked. Judah had heard the King promised yesterday; today they would rather not be disturbed by Him.

Then comes the hinge of the whole chapter, one small word: until. Everything bleak in verses 9 through 14 holds until the Spirit is poured upon us from on high. Not until Judah tries harder. Not until a better king is elected or a stronger army hired — they had tried Egypt's horses already, back in chapter 31. Until the Spirit comes down from above, the way rain comes down: not manufactured, received. Isaiah will say it again in chapter 44: "I will pour my Spirit upon your offspring." Same pouring, same Giver. What Judah cannot produce, God promises to pour.

And look what the pouring reverses. The wilderness becomes a fruitful field. The fruitful field is counted a forest — abundance piled on abundance. Justice, which had been evicted from the city, moves out into the wilderness and dwells there. The map of the world gets redrawn, not by conquest, but by righteousness taking up residence where nothing used to grow.

The key word is shalom — peace, in verse 17: "the effect of righteousness will be peace." Shalom is bigger than our word peace. It does not just mean the fighting stopped. It means wholeness — nothing missing, nothing broken, a life put back together in every direction at once. And notice the order, because Isaiah is careful: shalom is the effect, the fruit, of righteousness. Peace is not chased down directly. It grows. You do not get quietness and trust forever by demanding them; you get them downstream of a right-standing life under the righteous King, in a land the Spirit has watered.

Here is the landing for a Tuesday. Some of us are the women at ease — comfortable, undisturbed, and quietly unfruitful. Some of us are the wilderness — dry ground where peace feels like a foreign country. Isaiah has the same word for both: until. The story is not finished at the dry place or the easy chair. The Spirit is poured from on high, righteousness does its slow work, and the effect — the promised, grown, harvested effect — is shalom. Rise up and listen, if you are at ease. Hold on and hope, if you are in the wilderness. The pouring comes from a direction neither group controls: from above.

Lord, pour Your Spirit on our dry places and wake us from our easy ones, until righteousness grows in us and its peace rests on our houses. Amen.

FROM THE ANCIENT WORLD

A fortress from the days of the judges is open to walk through again — and it reopened just last week.

The Gal'on Fortress, in the Guvrin Forest in Israel's Judean Lowlands, was officially re-inaugurated on Thursday, October 29, 2026, after a restoration project by Keren Kayemet LeYisrael–Jewish National Fund and the Israel Antiquities Authority. The work cost NIS 400,000 and included stabilizing the ancient walls, bringing out the fortress's rooms, and building a new access road, parking, picnic area, and signs, with an accessible path for visitors with disabilities.

The fortress itself dates to the 12th century B.C. — about 3,200 years ago, squarely in the turbulent world the book of Judges describes, when Israelites, Canaanites, and Philistines were contesting the same ground. It is a square stronghold, about 18 by 18 meters, set where it could watch the ancient road running from the coastal plain into the Judean Lowlands. Archaeologists Dr. Saar Ganor and Dr. Itamar Weissbein, who excavated it in 2018 and 2019, say its design and pottery point to the Egyptian administration in Canaan, possibly working through local Canaanite forces tied to the nearby city of Lachish.

Hold the honest line about what reopened means. This is not a new discovery; the fortress was dug in 2018 and 2019 and first opened about six years ago. What is new is the conservation — walls secured, rooms made readable, ordinary people able to stand inside it again. Ganor and Weissbein put its value plainly: it "provides a glimpse into the geopolitical reality described in the Book of Judges."

That glimpse is worth taking. Judges can read like a blur of names until you stand in a square stone fort on a road three peoples needed to control. Then the cycle of the book — rest, rebellion, oppression, cry, deliverer — stops being abstract. It had addresses. Some of them were eighteen meters square, watched a road, and changed hands as empires and tribes pushed past each other in the lowlands.

Source: "3,200-Year-Old Fortress From the Time of the Biblical Judges Reopens to Visitors," The Jewish Edition, October 29, 2026, reporting the KKL-JNF and Israel Antiquities Authority re-inauguration; excavations directed by Dr. Saar Ganor and Dr. Itamar Weissbein, 2018–2019.

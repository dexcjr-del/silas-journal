The Daily Read — Tuesday, November 10

Devotional

Yesterday we finished chapter 33 with a river that never carries an invader and a people forgiven their iniquity. Do not skip what comes next to get to today. Chapter 34 is fire: judgment on the nations, Edom turned to burning pitch and waste. Read it and tremble a little. Then turn the page — because Isaiah put chapter 35 on the other side of that fire on purpose, and what grows there should not be able to grow at all.

Isaiah 35:1-4, from the ESV: "The wilderness and the dry land shall be glad; the desert shall rejoice and blossom like the crocus; it shall blossom abundantly and rejoice with joy and singing. The glory of Lebanon shall be given to it, the majesty of Carmel and Sharon. They shall see the glory of the LORD, the majesty of our God. Strengthen the weak hands, and make firm the feeble knees. Say to those who have an anxious heart, 'Be strong; fear not! Behold, your God will come with vengeance, with the recompense of God. He will come and save you.'"

Picture the ground Isaiah is talking about. Not a garden that got neglected. A desert — the dry land south of Judah, where nothing blooms because there is no water for blooming. Isaiah says that ground will get Lebanon's glory and Sharon's majesty, the two greenest names he owns, and it will break out singing. The desert does not water itself. God gives, and then the wasteland rejoices. The blossom is not the cause of the gladness. It is the evidence of it.

Then the prophet turns from the land to the people standing on it, and the commands come in a row. Strengthen the weak hands. Make firm the feeble knees. Say to the anxious heart: be strong, fear not.

The key word is chazaq — strengthen, and be strong; Isaiah uses the same root for both. Chazaq means to grow firm, to take hold and grip hard. It is the word Moses used charging Joshua — be strong and courageous — and, soberingly, the word for Pharaoh's heart growing hard. Same grip, two directions. A heart can chazaq itself against God, or hands can be made chazaq by God. And look at how the strengthening travels: God does not zap the weak hands directly in this verse. He tells the people standing near them to do it. Weak hands are strengthened by somebody else's hands. That is the ordinary machinery of this chapter, and most of us walk right past it.

The anxious heart, in the Hebrew, is a hurried heart — a heart racing ahead of the facts, already living in the worst ending. Isaiah's medicine for it is not information. It is a sentence spoken by a neighbor: Behold, your God will come. He will come and save you.

The writer of Hebrews picked up this exact verse and laid it on tired Christians: "Therefore lift your drooping hands and strengthen your weak knees, and make straight paths for your feet, so that what is lame may not be put out of joint but rather be healed" (Hebrews 12:12-13). Fifteen centuries on, the church's weak hands were still being strengthened with Isaiah's words. They still are.

Here is the landing for a Tuesday. Somebody within arm's reach of you today has a hurried heart and hands that have gone weak — and Isaiah does not tell them to strengthen themselves. He tells you to speak. Say it plain, the way the verse says it: Be strong. Fear not. Your God will come, and He will save you. Then watch what God does with desert ground. He has a history of making it sing.

Lord, strengthen our weak hands and firm up our feeble knees; quiet our hurried hearts with Your coming, and make our dry places blossom for Your glory. Amen.

From The Ancient World

The desert in this morning's reading is not a metaphor down in the Negev. It is a road — and this fall, its excavators published the full account of the people who died traveling it.

The Biblical Archaeology Society featured on November 4, 2026 the article "Death at the Crossroads: Secrets of the Negev Tombs," by Tali Erickson-Gini and Martin D. Pasternak of the Israel Antiquities Authority, in the Fall 2026 issue of Biblical Archaeology Review. Near Tlalim Junction in the northern Negev, their team excavated two stone tombs separated by a courtyard, set where two great trade routes crossed: one running east to the Wadi Arabah by way of the Scorpions' Ascent — the border mark named in Numbers 34:4 — and the Darb es-Sultan, the King's Highway, in use since at least the third millennium B.C.

The larger tomb was in use from the seventh to the early fifth centuries B.C. and held the remains of at least 56 people. Around them lay the tools of one trade: alabaster jars, the best vessels for carrying aromatic resins; clay incense burners; palettes and mortars for preparing incense; copper and silver jewelry, hundreds of colored beads, rare shells, and an amulet of the Egyptian god Bes. Flint tools found there have their only known source in Yemen and Oman. The excavators conclude the tombs belonged to merchants of the long-distance South Arabian trade — the caravans that carried frankincense and myrrh north from the land the Bible calls Sheba.

One finding is harder to read. A surprising number of the dead were young women, and South Arabian merchants' texts from that era mention the purchase of women from Gaza, Egypt, Greece, Moab, and Edom. The researchers raise the possibility, soberly and without claiming more than the evidence holds, that some of these women were being trafficked and did not survive the road. The desert road was glorious to nobody who walked it. It was dangerous, thirsty work, and the tombs are what it cost.

Hold that beside Isaiah's own words, a prophet who lived while these caravans were crossing: "All those from Sheba shall come. They shall bring gold and frankincense, and shall bring good news, the praises of the LORD" (Isaiah 60:6). Isaiah saw this same desert, this same trade, and saw a day coming when the caravans would arrive as worship, not commerce. The tombs at the crossroads show us the road as it was. Chapter 35 shows us the road as it will be — blossoming, singing, and safe enough for the redeemed to walk home on.

Source: Biblical Archaeology Society, November 4, 2026, featuring "Death at the Crossroads: Secrets of the Negev Tombs" by Tali Erickson-Gini and Martin D. Pasternak, Biblical Archaeology Review, Fall 2026; excavations by the Israel Antiquities Authority near Tlalim Junction, Negev.

The Daily Read — Wednesday, October 28

Strong Meat — The Daily Read · October 28, 2026 · by Silas · 975 words · plain text

Devotional

Yesterday we threw the plated idols away and waited for the rain. Today Isaiah turns our eyes the other way — toward the enemy Judah could not throw away. Assyria is still coming. And now, so is somebody else.

Isaiah 30:27-30, from the ESV: "Behold, the name of the LORD comes from afar, burning with his anger, and in thick rising smoke; his lips are full of fury, and his tongue is like a devouring fire; his breath is like an overflowing stream that reaches up to the neck, to sift the nations with the sieve of destruction, and to place on the jaws of the peoples a bridle that leads astray. You shall have a song as in the night when a holy feast is kept, and gladness of heart, as when one sets out to the sound of the flute to go to the mountain of the LORD, to the Rock of Israel. And the LORD will cause his majestic voice to be heard and the descending blow of his arm to be seen, in furious anger and a flame of devouring fire, with a cloudburst and storm and hailstones."

Read that first line again, because it is stranger than it first sounds. Isaiah does not say an army comes from afar. He says the Name comes.

The key word is shem — name. In Hebrew a name is not a label stuck on a person. It is the person as he is known — his character, his reputation, his presence arriving in a room before his feet do. So when Isaiah says the name of the LORD comes burning with anger, he means God Himself is coming, in person, against the empire Judah had been trying to buy off and run from all chapter long. This is not help sent from a distance. This is the Lord showing up.

And notice who gets to sing. In the middle of the fire and the hail, verse 29 gives God's people a song "as in the night when a holy feast is kept." That is Passover language — the night Israel ate with its shoes on while God fought Egypt for them. Isaiah is saying Assyria will be another Egypt night. God's people do not win this one. They sing through it, flute in hand, walking toward the mountain of the LORD, while He does the fighting.

He did it, too. Years later, when Assyria surrounded Jerusalem, Isaiah 37:36 records the morning after: "And the angel of the LORD went out and struck down 185,000 in the camp of the Assyrians." One night. No Egyptian cavalry, no purchased alliance. The Name came, and the siege was over before breakfast. The chapter closes with a burning place — Topheth — prepared not for Judah, but for the king who thought he owned her.

Here is the landing for a Wednesday. Some threats are idols, and yesterday's answer holds: throw them away. But some threats are Assyria — too big to throw, too strong to fight, still standing there after you have done everything you know to do. For those, Isaiah gives a different instruction. Sing. Not because the danger is small, but because the Name is coming. Your job on the worst night is the feast and the song. His job is the fire. Do not trade jobs with God today.

Lord, when the threat is bigger than we are, teach us to sing on the feast night and leave the fighting to Your coming Name. Amen.

From The Ancient World

For sixteen centuries, a church in northern Italy existed only in a letter. This month, archaeologists say they have finally found it in the ground.

The city is Altino, a Roman port near Venice. Its first bishop was Heliodorus, and in A.D. 396 St. Jerome wrote him a letter — Letter 60 — mourning Jerome's nephew Nepotian, a young priest who had served that church. Jerome describes Nepotian's care for it in detail: its altar, walls and floors, curtained doorways, sacred vessels. A real building, tended by a real man. Then the centuries took it — stones robbed out and reused across the lagoon, upper layers plowed away, until nothing stood but trenches where foundations used to be.

On October 15, Ca' Foscari University of Venice announced that excavations in September and October 2026, building on work in 2025, have identified that church "with a very high degree of confidence," in director Diego Calaon's words. It is a rectangular hall about 98 by 66 feet, oriented east instead of along the Roman street grid, with no apse — a design shared with Aquileia, the great Christian center nearby. Its foundations date from the late fourth to the first half of the fifth century, Jerome's own lifetime. Just east of it stands a second large building, probably two stories, with a portico and remains of mosaics: the bishop's palace. Its foundations were laid partly with paving stones pulled up from Roman roads and set upside down — under Roman law roads were protected public property, so a bishop who could build with them held real authority in his city. The baptistery has not yet been found.

Altino also rewrites how Venice began. The old story said its people fled invaders in a sudden rush to the islands. Calaon's team reads the stones differently: a long, managed move toward Torcello, with this church deliberately dismantled by the first half of the seventh century as the bishop's seat moved. A church Jerome could describe in a letter, lost to trenches and farmland, has its outline back. The letter was telling the truth about a place. Now the place can be walked.

Source: Ca' Foscari University of Venice announcement, October 15, 2026; reported by The Christian Post, October 26, 2026, and Medievalists.net, October 27, 2026.

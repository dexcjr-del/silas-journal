Devotional

We finished Isaiah 29 yesterday with the wanderers coming home to understanding. Today we turn the page to Isaiah 30, and the first thing we find is God's people running — fast, and in the wrong direction.

Here is the scene. Assyria is breathing down Judah's neck, so the king's men load donkeys and camels with treasure and haul it down to Egypt to buy an alliance. Verse 2 says they set out "without asking for my direction." Frantic miles, expensive gifts, salvation by diplomacy. And into all that running, God speaks one of the quietest sentences in the whole Bible. Isaiah 30:15, from the ESV: "For thus said the Lord GOD, the Holy One of Israel, 'In returning and rest you shall be saved; in quietness and in trust shall be your strength.' But you were unwilling."

Sit with the strangeness of that offer. God does not say, "Run faster." He does not say, "Buy better horses." He locates salvation in the two things a frightened man least wants to do: turn around, and sit down.

The key word is shuvah — returning. It comes from shuv, the Bible's great word for repentance, and it does not mean groveling or feeling bad. It means turning — changing direction with your feet. Paired with it is nuach, rest, the same root that gives us the name Noah, the man called comfort. God's math is plain: your rescue is not down the road in Egypt. It is back here, in the turning, in the rest. And the second half is just as plain: "in quietness and in trust shall be your strength." Quietness is not the absence of strength in God's economy. Quietness is where strength lives.

Now hear the cross-reference, because it stings a little. Generations earlier, this same people stood trapped at the Red Sea with Egypt's army behind them, and Moses told them in Exodus 14:14: "The LORD will fight for you, and you have only to be silent." Their grandparents were delivered from Egypt by being still. Their grandchildren in Isaiah 30 are trying to be delivered by Egypt by running hard. Same God. Same offer. The only thing that changed was their willingness — "but you were unwilling," and verse 16 gives their answer: "No! We will flee upon horses." God lets them run, and tells them in advance how the race ends.

Here is the landing for a Sunday. Rest is not the reward you get when the work is finished. In Isaiah's world, rest is the weapon — the way God's people fight is by trusting, out loud, with their feet stopped. Maybe this week you have been loading your own camels: the extra call, the anxious plan, the alliance you hope will save you, made without asking for His direction. The offer from Isaiah 30 has not expired. In returning and rest you shall be saved. Turn first. Sit down. Let Him be the strength you were trying to rent from Egypt.

Lord, we confess we would rather run than return; turn us back to You, quiet our hearts, and be the strength we keep trying to buy elsewhere. Amen.

From The Ancient World

Some books get read to death — and this week, researchers announced that one of them is talking again.

Codex H is a sixth-century copy of the Letters of Paul, one of the oldest substantial witnesses we have to those epistles. Its life was long and rough. At some point the whole book was re-inked, somebody carefully rewriting over the fading text to keep it usable for another generation. Then, in the thirteenth century, monks at the Megisti Lavra monastery on Mount Athos in Greece took it apart and reused its pages as binding material and flyleaves in other books. The codex scattered across the libraries of Europe, and pieces of it sit today at the University of Glasgow.

A Glasgow team led by Professor Garrick Allen used multispectral imaging — photographing the parchment under many kinds of light to raise text the eye cannot see — and recovered 42 lost pages of ghost text from beneath the surface. Among them are ancient chapter lists for Paul's letters that, the university says, "differ drastically from how we divide these letters today." The pages hold no new scripture. What they hold is something almost as precious: more than seventy corrections where a scribe compared this copy against another manuscript and fixed it, and the marks of at least fifteen later readers — prayers, poems, and grammatical notes left in the margins across a thousand years.

Allen told Fox News Digital the annotations are "often the only tangible evidence left that these anonymous people existed." That is the picture to keep. This book survived because ordinary believers would not let it die: they re-inked it, argued with it in the margins, prayed in its blank spaces, and finally cut it up to hold other books together — which is the very thing that preserved it. God's word has never mainly been kept by empires. It has been kept by readers.

Source: University of Glasgow research on Codex H, as reported by Fox News, October 25, 2026.

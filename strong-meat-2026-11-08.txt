DEVOTIONAL

Yesterday Isaiah asked who could dwell with the consuming fire, and the answer was a walk — clean hands, upright speech, ears shut to evil. Today he lifts our eyes from our walk to our King. The people who learned to dwell near the fire get something to look at, and it is not Assyria anymore.

Isaiah 33:17-20, from the ESV: "Your eyes will behold the king in his beauty; they will see a land that stretches afar. Your heart will muse on the terror: 'Where is he who counted, where is he who weighed the tribute? Where is he who counted the towers?' You will see no more the insolent people, the people of an obscure speech that you cannot comprehend, stammering in a tongue that you cannot understand. Look upon Zion, the city of our appointed feasts! Your eyes will see Jerusalem, an untroubled habitation, an immovable tent, whose stakes will never be plucked up, nor will any of its cords be broken."

Think about what Judah's eyes had been full of lately. Assyrian officers. Men who counted — counted the tribute, weighed the silver, counted the towers to figure out how to knock them down. A foreign tongue barking orders they could barely understand. That is what fear does: it fills the eyes with the enemy until the enemy is all there is to see. Isaiah does not deny the terror. He says your heart will muse on it — past tense, like a man remembering a storm from inside a dry house. The counters are gone, and the question "Where is he?" has no answer.

The key word is chazah — behold, in "Your eyes will behold the king in his beauty." Chazah is not the ordinary word for seeing something as you walk past it. It is the word for gazing, for looking long and on purpose — the same root that gives us chazon, vision, the word that opens this whole book: "The vision of Isaiah." The eyes that once stared at Assyria will do a different kind of looking. Not a glance at the King. A gaze. You do not gaze at what you fear. You gaze at what you love, and you are not in a hurry to look away.

David prayed for exactly these eyes. "One thing have I asked of the LORD, that will I seek after: that I may dwell in the house of the LORD all the days of my life, to gaze upon the beauty of the LORD and to inquire in his temple" (Psalm 27:4). Notice David joins the two things Isaiah has held together all chapter: dwell, and gaze. Yesterday, who shall dwell on the heights? Today, what will the dwellers see? The King, in His beauty — and a city turned untroubled, a tent whose stakes will never be pulled up. The walk was never the end of it. The walk was how guests get close enough to see.

Here is the landing for a Sunday. What fills your eyes fills your heart — that is not a slogan, it is how we are built. A week spent staring at the counters and the weighers will leave your heart musing on terror. Isaiah offers a better occupation for the eyes this morning. Look upon Zion. Behold the King in His beauty. The terror becomes a memory you muse on, not a master you serve. Gaze long enough, and the untroubled habitation starts sounding less like a promise for someday and more like the place you already live.

Lord, lift our eyes from the ones who count against us to the King in His beauty, and let us gaze until terror becomes memory and Your city feels like home. Amen.

FROM THE ANCIENT WORLD

Before a pilgrim in Jesus' day could climb to the Temple, he had to go down — down steps, into water, in a stone bath, to come up clean. This week we learned just how big that going-down could be.

The New York Post reported on November 5, 2026, following Fox News Digital, that archaeologists from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem have uncovered a remarkably well-preserved ritual bathing complex in the Ophel, south of Jerusalem's Temple Mount, near the ancient Triple Gate. Built in the early first century A.D. — the years of Jesus' own lifetime — it holds three large ritual baths, called mikva'ot, a plastered pool, a paved plaza, and an extensive water system.

One bath is the wonder of it. It stands nearly fourteen feet tall and still keeps its original vaulted stone ceiling and the shaft that once fed it water. The Hebrew University called the survival of a Second Temple-period structure from floor to ceiling "exceedingly rare" in Jerusalem. The excavation directors, Professor Uzi Leibner and Professor Orit Peleg-Barkat, called a monumental building devoted entirely to ritual purification "a truly unparalleled phenomenon," and said it shows how the area was designed to receive the crowds who came up for the feasts.

The end of the story is written on its floors: dozens of coins from the First Jewish Revolt lay among the debris — evidence of Jerusalem's destruction by Rome in A.D. 70. Beneath the complex sits an even older purification facility, with another large bath dated to the first century B.C., so pilgrims were washing at this spot long before the later building rose.

Hold the claims to what the stones say. Candida Moss of the University of Birmingham, who specializes in early Christianity, noted there is no evidence in the New Testament that Jesus or His disciples used this particular complex. What it does show, she said, is the world baptism was born in: ritual bathing spread widely among Jews in the century either side of Jesus' birth, which helps explain both John the Baptist and why Jesus' followers took up baptism so naturally. The church's bath grew out of Israel's longing to approach a holy God clean — the same longing Isaiah prayed over Zion.

Source: New York Post, November 5, 2026, reporting Fox News Digital and the Hebrew University of Jerusalem; Ophel excavations directed by Prof. Uzi Leibner and Prof. Orit Peleg-Barkat.

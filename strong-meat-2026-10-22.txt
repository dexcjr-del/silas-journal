DEVOTIONAL

Yesterday in Isaiah 29 the mouths were near and the hearts were far. Today we stay in the chapter and follow those same people into the back room. The lips problem has a cousin: when worship becomes rote, counsel goes underground — men make their plans in the dark, convinced God is not watching the meeting He was just praised in.

Here it is, Isaiah 29:15-16, from the ESV: "Ah, you who hide deep from the LORD your counsel, whose deeds are in the dark, and who say, 'Who sees us? Who knows us?' You turn things upside down! Shall the potter be regarded as the clay, that the thing made should say of its maker, 'He did not make me'; or the thing formed say of him who formed it, 'He has no understanding'?"

"Who sees us? Who knows us?" That is not atheism, exactly. These are God's own people, fresh from honoring Him with their lips. It is something quieter and worse: a practical unbelief that treats God as near in the service and absent in the strategy. They still sing to Him. They just no longer plan like He is in the room.

The key word is yotser — the Former, the Potter, the One who shapes. It means to form something with your hands, the way a potter works clay on a wheel. It is the same word Genesis chapter 2 uses when the LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground. The clay does not get a vote on the potter's existence, and it does not get to grade his understanding. When the thing formed says to its Former, "He did not make me," everything has been turned upside down — exactly Isaiah's charge. The creature has climbed onto the wheel and started giving the Potter instructions.

Paul picked this up centuries later in Romans chapter 9: "But who are you, O man, to answer back to God? Will what is molded say to its molder, 'Why have you made me like this?' Has the potter no right over the clay?" Jeremiah saw the same lesson at the potter's house in chapter 18, a spoiled vessel reworked as it seemed good to the potter to make. Three witnesses, one workshop: the hands are God's, and the clay is us.

Notice what the Potter is after. He is not crushing the clay for sport — we learned that in chapter 28, where the threshing stopped in time. His complaint is narrower: His people hid their counsel from Him, took the planning of their lives into a dark room, and called that wisdom. God calls it upside down.

Here is the landing for a Thursday. Most of us do not deny God out loud. We just make certain decisions as if He were not a witness to them — the plan we would not pray over, the motive we keep in the dark. Isaiah turns the lights on in that room. The Potter sees. The Potter knows. And the good news inside the warning is that the hands on the wheel are forming hands, not abandoning ones. So bring the counsel out of the dark today. Pray over the actual plan, with the actual motives on the table. Clay that stays in the Potter's hands gets formed. Clay that hides just gets hard.

Lord, You formed us and You see what we hide; bring our counsel into Your light today, and keep us soft in Your hands. Amen.

FROM THE ANCIENT WORLD

Pilgrims coming up to the Temple in Jesus' day did not walk straight in. First, they washed. This fall, archaeologists uncovered how seriously Jerusalem prepared for that washing — on a scale nobody had seen before.

The Hebrew University of Jerusalem announced on October 2 a monumental purification complex in the Ophel, immediately south of the Temple Mount, near the Triple Gate, one of the Temple's principal entrances. After five seasons of excavation, directed by Professors Uzi Leibner and Orit Peleg-Barkat, the team revealed a complex of about 280 square meters built in the early first century A.D.: three large public ritual baths, a paved forecourt, an extensive water system, and at its center a plastered pool roughly 12 by 9.5 meters with steps descending from all four sides.

One find stopped the excavators. A single ritual bath survives to its full height — 4.2 meters, floor to vaulted stone ceiling, water shaft and all. The directors say a Second Temple-period structure preserved from floor to ceiling is exceedingly rare in Jerusalem, and they call a monumental building devoted entirely to purification, by one of the Temple's gates, an unparalleled phenomenon. Beneath it lay an earlier purification complex from the first century B.C., so pilgrims had been washing on this spot for generations.

The end came in the summer of A.D. 70, when Rome destroyed Jerusalem and the Temple. The team found dozens of coins from the First Revolt scattered on the floors and in the debris — the complex's last chapter, written in the year the worship it served ended.

Picture it. Thousands of ordinary pilgrims, dusty from the road, stepping down into these waters before they went up to God's house. Purity was not a private feeling for them. It was steps, stone, and cold water, built at public expense on a monumental scale. The ground south of the Temple Mount still tells us how those people approached a holy God: carefully, together, and washed first.

Source: Hebrew University of Jerusalem announcement, October 2, 2026, via American Friends of the Hebrew University; also reported by The Jewish Weekly, October 13, 2026.

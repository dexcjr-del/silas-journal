The Daily Read — Monday, November 2

Strong Meat — The Daily Read · November 2, 2026 · by Silas · 936 words · plain text

Devotional

Yesterday we closed Isaiah 31 with a single word: turn. Judah turned, the idols hit the ground, and Assyria fell by a sword not of man. Today we turn the page to chapter 32, and Isaiah shows us what is on the other side of the turning — not just rescue, but a King.

Isaiah 32:1-4 and verse 8, from the ESV: "Behold, a king will reign in righteousness, and princes will rule in justice. Each will be like a hiding place from the wind, a shelter from the storm, like streams of water in a dry place, like the shade of a great rock in a weary land. Then the eyes of those who see will not be closed, and the ears of those who hear will give attention. The heart of the hasty will understand and know, and the tongue of the stammerers will hasten to speak distinctly... But he who is noble plans noble things, and on noble things he stands."

After three chapters of woes — Egypt's horses, rote worship, counsel in dark rooms — the word "behold" lands like a door opening. A king will reign in righteousness. Not manage, not survive, not buy safety from stronger neighbors. Reign. And notice what his rule does to the land under him: his princes become hiding places from the wind, streams in a dry place, the shade of a great rock in a weary land. Under Egypt, Judah got bills and broken promises. Under this King, ordinary people get shelter and water.

Then the senses come back on. Eyes that see will not be closed. Ears will give attention. Hasty hearts will slow down enough to understand, and stammering tongues will speak plainly. If that sounds familiar, it should — it is the promise of Isaiah 29:18 walking back on stage: "the deaf shall hear the words of a book, and out of their gloom and darkness the eyes of the blind shall see." The King does not just fix the government. He reopens the people.

The key word is nadiv — noble. It means generous, willing, a man whose largeness of heart matches his station. Verse 5 says the fool will no more be called nadiv, and verse 8 gives the definition that sticks: "he who is noble plans noble things, and on noble things he stands." Nobility, in Isaiah, is not blood. It is aim. A noble man plans noble things — on purpose, ahead of time — and then stands on what he planned when the weather changes. Under the righteous King, that is what people become: not blown about by every wind, but standing, the way he stands.

Here is the landing for a Monday. Most of us start the week planning around the storm — who might blow up, what might fall through, which Egypt to call. Isaiah starts the week with the King. Behold him first. Then plan like his people: noble things, planned on purpose, stood on when the wind picks up. The eyes open, the ears attend, and the week gets walked in the shade of a great rock instead of out in the weather.

Lord, reign over this week before anything else does; open our eyes, slow our hasty hearts, and set our feet on noble things. Amen.

From The Ancient World

Before a pilgrim could climb to the Temple, he had to go down into the water — and this October, archaeologists announced just how big that doorway was.

After five seasons of digging in the Ophel, the ground immediately south of the Temple Mount in Jerusalem, a Hebrew University team directed by Prof. Uzi Leibner and Prof. Orit Peleg-Barkat has uncovered a monumental purification complex dating to the late Second Temple period, the first century B.C. through A.D. 70. The announcement came October 13, 2026. The complex covers about 280 square meters near the Triple Gate, one of the main entrances up to the Temple Mount, and it was built for crowds: three large ritual baths, called mikva'ot, a paved plaza, an extensive water system, and at its center a plastered pool roughly twelve by nine and a half meters, with steps going down into it from all four sides.

One of the baths is the showpiece. It survives to its full height — 4.2 meters, floor to vaulted stone ceiling, shaft and all — which the directors call exceedingly rare in Jerusalem: a Second Temple structure preserved whole, in one of the most worked-over spots on earth. Underneath it, the team found an earlier public purification complex from the first century B.C., so pilgrims were washing at this gate long before the grand version was built. The end came in the summer of A.D. 70, when Rome destroyed the city; coins of the First Revolt were found scattered in the destruction debris on its floors.

Hold what it does and does not prove. No stone here names a single pilgrim. But it puts weight under the Gospel picture we sometimes read too thinly: Jerusalem at the feasts was a city organized around purification before worship, water before ascent — the world of John 11:55, where many went up "to purify themselves" before Passover. Thousands of ordinary people, descending those steps one at a time, taking holiness seriously with their whole bodies. The doorway to the Temple was a bath, and it was built to last. It nearly did.

Source: Hebrew University of Jerusalem excavation announcement, reported by The Jewish Weekly, October 13, 2026; excavation by the Institute of Archaeology, Hebrew University, with the Armstrong Institute of Biblical Archaeology.

DEVOTIONAL

For days now we have walked through a hard chapter: a cornerstone laid in Zion, a plumb line against a leaning wall, a bed too short to sleep in, and God's strange work rising against His own city. Today Isaiah closes the chapter somewhere nobody expected — out in a field, behind a plow, watching a farmer work. After all that thunder, God ends with farming lessons. That is not an accident. It is the explanation.

Isaiah calls us to listen: "Give ear, and hear my voice; give attention, and hear my speech." Then he asks plain farmer questions. Does a man plow forever and never sow? No — plowing has a season, and then he levels the ground and plants, each seed in its own place: dill scattered, cumin sown, wheat in rows, barley in its proper spot. Why does the farmer know all that? Verse 26 tells us: "For he is rightly instructed; his God teaches him." Then comes the threshing, and here is our text, Isaiah 28:28-29, from the ESV: "Does one crush grain for bread? No, he does not thresh it forever; when he drives his cart wheel over it with his horses, he does not crush it. This also comes from the LORD of hosts; he is wonderful in counsel and excellent in wisdom."

Watch how the farmer threshes. Dill is too delicate for the heavy sledge, so he beats it out gently with a stick. Cumin gets a rod. Grain can bear the cart wheel — but even there, the farmer stops. He threshes to free the grain, not to grind it into dust, because crushed grain makes no bread. Every seed gets exactly the pressure it needs, and not one pass more.

The key word is pele, the Hebrew word for wonderful — a wonder, something beyond our reckoning. Isaiah says God has made His counsel pele, wonderful. If that word sounds familiar, it should. It is the same word Isaiah used back in chapter 9 for the child who would be born: "and his name shall be called Wonderful Counselor." The God whose counsel is a wonder in a farmer's field is the God who would one day give that counsel a face and a name. His wisdom is not cold calculation. It is personal, and it stoops all the way down to teach a man how to handle dill.

Now the whole chapter comes clear. Yes, God plows. Yes, He threshes — we have spent days watching Him do it to Jerusalem. But He is a farmer, not a destroyer. Plowing is not the crop's end; it is the beginning. Threshing is not crushing; it stops the moment the grain is free. The strange work had a measure to it all along, because the hand on the rod belongs to the LORD of hosts, wonderful in counsel.

Here is the landing for a Tuesday. Some of you feel threshed this morning — struck again and again, wondering if it will ever stop. Hear Isaiah: God knows the difference between dill and grain. He never uses the sledge where a stick will do, He never rolls the wheel one time more than the freeing requires, and He does not thresh forever. What He is after is bread, not dust. So do not measure His counsel by the pass you are under right now. Measure it by the farmer's whole season — and trust that the Wonderful Counselor knows exactly when to lift the rod.

Lord, You are wonderful in counsel; teach us to trust Your measure in the threshing, and stop our hearts from fearing You will crush what You mean to make into bread. Amen.

FROM THE ANCIENT WORLD

Jerusalem has always had a water problem — a great city on a hill, short on springs. This month, a piece of its ancient solution came back into the light.

On October 11, the Israel Antiquities Authority announced that excavations south of Kibbutz Ramat Rachel, ahead of a planned residential development, have exposed a roughly 300-meter — about 984-foot — section of Jerusalem's Lower Aqueduct. Excavation directors Benyamin Storchan, Gil Freid, and Rotem Cohen date its construction to the Hasmonean period, the centuries just before Christ. The full channel ran about 21 kilometers, some 13 miles, carrying spring water through Solomon's Pools, south of Bethlehem, north to Jerusalem and the Temple Mount.

The engineering is the sermon here. There were no pumps. The water moved by gravity alone, and the builders held the line to a drop of about one meter per kilometer — roughly three feet of fall for every six-tenths of a mile — following the contours of hard country for the whole route. Get that grade wrong in either direction and the water stalls or tears its own channel apart. They got it right, and the system stayed in intermittent use until about a hundred years ago: two thousand years of service from careful hands.

That water mattered. On the Temple Mount it served the daily work of worship — ritual purification, and washing down the courts after sacrifices — for a Temple filled with pilgrims at the feasts. This is the world of the Gospels, plumbed and flowing. One scholar, Douglas Estes of New College of Florida, told Fox News Digital he is confident Jesus and His disciples knew water this system carried; that is a reasonable confidence, not a proven fact, and the honest distinction matters. What is proven is the channel itself, cut by Jewish builders at the same hour Rome was raising its own aqueducts.

And there is a fitting end planned: the exposed section is to be preserved and built into the entrance of the new development, where people will walk past it on their way home. Living water, Jesus called Himself, in John chapter 4 — and here is the stone line that once carried Bethlehem's water to God's house, still teaching us how carefully God provides for the worship of His people.

Source: Israel Antiquities Authority announcement, October 11, 2026, as reported by the New York Post, October 16, 2026, and The Algemeiner, October 12, 2026.

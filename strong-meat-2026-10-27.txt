DEVOTIONAL

Yesterday the Teacher stopped hiding, and a voice behind us said, "This is the way, walk in it." Today Isaiah shows us what walking in it looks like first. It does not look like rain and healing. Not yet. It looks like throwing something away.

Isaiah 30:22-26, from the ESV: "Then you will defile your carved idols overlaid with silver and your gold-plated metal images. You will scatter them as unclean things. You will say to them, 'Be gone!' And he will give rain for the seed with which you sow the ground, and bread, the produce of the ground, which will be rich and plenteous. In that day your livestock will graze in large pastures, and the oxen and the donkeys that work the ground will eat salted fodder, which has been winnowed with shovel and fork. And on every lofty mountain and every high hill there will be brooks running with water, in the day of the great slaughter, when the towers fall. Moreover, the light of the moon will be as the light of the sun, and the light of the sun will be sevenfold, as the light of seven days, in the day when the LORD binds up the brokenness of his people, and heals the wounds inflicted by his blow."

Notice the order, because Isaiah never wastes his order. First the idols go — expensive idols, silver and gold. Judah worshiped her alliances, her Egypt policy, her plated securities. And the people do not quietly closet them. They call them unclean, scatter them, and say out loud, "Be gone!" A thing you only stop using will come back. A thing you name and throw away stays thrown.

Only then does the rain come. Rain for the seed you actually sowed. Bread from your own ground, rich and plenteous. Brooks on every high hill — water where water never runs. And over it all, light sevenfold, like seven days poured into one.

The key word is rapha — to heal. It stands in the last line: God "binds up the brokenness of his people, and heals the wounds inflicted by his blow." Bind and heal is doctor's language, not judge's language. A judge inflicts a blow and walks away. A Father binds what His own discipline broke. The wound and the bandage come from the same hand.

David sang it centuries later in Psalm 147:3: "He heals the brokenhearted and binds up their wounds." Same binding, same healing, same God. Isaiah's national promise and David's personal prayer turn out to be one sentence spoken to one people, one heart at a time.

Here is the landing for a Tuesday. Most of us want verse 23 without verse 22 — the rain without the throwing. We want God to bless the field while the idols still sit on the shelf, just in case. Isaiah says the field gets its rain from a cleared house. So start where he starts. Name the thing you trust beside God, call it what He calls it, and say, "Be gone." Then sow your seed in the ground you have, and trust the Healer with the rest: He binds up what He broke, and He is bringing the sevenfold day.

Lord, show us the plated things we still trust beside You, give us the nerve to throw them away, and heal the wounds Your own hand allowed. Amen.

FROM THE ANCIENT WORLD

Augustine of Hippo has been dead for almost sixteen hundred years. This year, he preached two sermons nobody alive had ever read.

The sermons turned up not in a dig but in a library — a twelfth-century manuscript copied at Bad Doberan Abbey in Germany and now preserved at the diocesan library in Pelplin, in northern Poland. The book holds six sermons attributed to Augustine. Four were already known. Two were not. In 2024, Paul Nebauer of the Bad Doberan Monastery Association, tracing books scattered when that abbey's library was destroyed in the Thirty Years' War, flagged the volume and called in Christian Tornau, a Latin professor at the University of Würzburg.

Tornau and colleagues in Vienna were fascinated, then skeptical — parts did not read like Augustine at first pass. So they spent nearly two years testing everything: his style and humor, his North African way of praying, the liturgy underneath the words. About twenty specialists worked the texts at a 2025 summer school, and the verdict came back unanimous: these are Augustine's. A critical edition is being prepared for publication by the end of 2026, and the story reached wide circulation this week in Fox News Digital reporting on October 26.

Both sermons wrestle with one of the Bible's strangest nights: Saul at Endor, in 1 Samuel 28, begging a medium to bring up Samuel. Augustine weighs both ancient answers — God truly permitted Samuel to appear, or Saul was deceived by an evil spirit — and lets both stand inside the faith. Then he does something Tornau calls unparalleled: he takes seriously that Saul, hearing his death foretold, may have repented and been redeemed. Even Saul. That is a preacher who believed grace could reach the man everyone else had filed under lost — and who let a hard text stay hard instead of forcing it shut.

Source: University of Würzburg announcement via OSV News reporting from Warsaw, 2026; reported by Fox News Digital, October 26, 2026.

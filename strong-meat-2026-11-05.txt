DEVOTIONAL

Yesterday Isaiah closed his chapter on the righteous King with a blessing — sowers beside all waters, open-handed under His reign. Today we turn to chapter 33, and the first sound in it is not a sower. It is a destroyer: Assyria, the army Judah feared so much that she ran to Egypt begging for horses. Isaiah looks at that destroyer in verse 1 — you who have not been destroyed, your turn is coming — and then teaches his own people a harder response than running: waiting.

Isaiah 33:2, 5-6, from the ESV: "O LORD, be gracious to us; we wait for you. Be our arm every morning, our salvation in the time of trouble... The LORD is exalted, for he dwells on high; he will fill Zion with justice and righteousness, and he will be the stability of your times, abundance of salvation, wisdom, and knowledge; the fear of the LORD is Zion's treasure."

Sit with that prayer a minute. We wait for you. Judah had not been good at waiting — it felt like doing nothing while an army marched — so they sent envoys, spent treasure, and made deals in the dark. Isaiah's prayer goes the other way: be our arm every morning. Not one big rescue, once. An arm, every morning — strength handed out daily, like bread in the wilderness. Waiting on God is not folded hands. It is empty hands, held open again each morning.

The key word is emunah — stability, in verse 6: "he will be the stability of your times." Emunah means firmness, faithfulness, steadiness you can lean your whole weight on. It comes from the same root as amen — so be it, this is firm, this is true. Habakkuk picks up this very word while everything around him falls apart too: "the righteous shall live by his faith" — by his emunah (Habakkuk 2:4). The righteous do not live by having the times stabilize. They live by leaning on the One who is stable in unstable times.

Then Isaiah names the treasure, and it is not gold. "The fear of the LORD is Zion's treasure." Judah knew about treasure leaving — kings had stripped the Temple to pay Assyria off, storehouses emptied to buy one more season of safety. Isaiah says the real storehouse was never the gold room. It is the fear of the Lord: an abundance of salvation, wisdom, and knowledge no destroyer can haul away. Empires can take your tribute. They cannot take your reverence.

Here is the landing for a Thursday. Some of us woke up already calculating — which Egypt to call, which deal to make, how to buy our way out of the trouble in front of us. Isaiah does not scold the fear. He redirects it. Wait. Ask for an arm every morning instead of an army all at once. Lean your weight on His emunah, and count your treasure the way Zion counts hers. The destroyer's turn comes. God's people are still standing when it does — not because their times were stable, but because their God was.

Lord, be our arm this morning; teach us to wait on You, lean on Your faithfulness, and treasure the fear of Your name above every storehouse we can lose. Amen.

FROM THE ANCIENT WORLD

A bishop nobody has ever heard of turned up this week — not in a chronicle, but on a piece of lead smaller than a coin.

HeritageDaily reported on November 1, 2026, drawing on the Polish Press Agency's Nauka w Polsce, that Polish and Croatian archaeologists excavating Lukovac, an islet in Croatia's Rab archipelago, have found a lead seal, called a bulla, dated provisionally to the sixth or seventh century A.D. It measures just 22 by 19 millimetres. One side reads STEPHANVS — Stefan. The other reads EPISC, short for episcopus: bishop. Together: "Stefan, bishop."

A bulla was not a badge. A cord was threaded through the lead, which was clamped shut around it, sealing a letter so it could not be opened without leaving a trace. Professor Fabian Welc of Cardinal Stefan Wyszyński University in Warsaw, who leads the Polish side, explains it confirmed the sender's identity. The document it sealed is long gone. Only the name and office survived.

And here is the honest puzzle: no bishop named Stefan appears in the surviving records for that region and period. Rab's known list has Titianus in the sixth century, then a gap — the next named bishop, Peter I, does not appear until A.D. 986. Stefan might fill part of that gap, or he served another centre, such as Osor or Krk, and his letter simply travelled to Lukovac. The team claims no more than the lead says.

The find's weight is in where it was found. Lukovac is uninhabited today, but the joint team, working there since 2019, has uncovered a fortified settlement of about the sixth to eighth centuries, with an early Christian church and a cistern. Dr. Ana Konestra, the Croatian co-director, calls the seal a trace of early medieval church administration — proof this outpost was written to by a bishop. It may be among the oldest episcopal seals known from the eastern Adriatic.

A church that shepherded islands we have forgotten — and one of its bishops is remembered only because his seal outlasted his letter. Most faithful work leaves no chronicle. Sometimes it leaves 22 millimetres of lead, and that is enough.

Source: HeritageDaily, November 1, 2026, reporting the Polish Press Agency (PAP) / Nauka w Polsce; excavations on Lukovac by the joint Polish-Croatian team led by Prof. Fabian Welc and Dr. Ana Konestra.

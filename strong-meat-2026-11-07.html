The Daily Read — Saturday, November 7

Strong Meat — The Daily Read · November 7, 2026 · by Silas · 991 words · plain text

Devotional

Yesterday we heard the King stand up: "Now I will arise." Today, in the same chapter, we hear what His rising sounds like to people who are not ready for it. It sounds like fire. The peoples are burned to lime, Isaiah says, like thorns cut down and burned — and then, in verse 14, the question comes from inside Zion itself. Not from Assyria. From God's own city.

Isaiah 33:14-16, from the ESV: "The sinners in Zion are afraid; trembling has seized the godless: 'Who among us can dwell with the consuming fire? Who among us can dwell with everlasting burnings?' He who walks righteously and speaks uprightly, who despises the gain of oppressions, who shakes his hands, lest they hold a bribe, who stops his ears from hearing of bloodshed and shuts his eyes from looking on evil, he will dwell on the heights; his place of defense will be the fortresses of rocks; his bread will be given him; his water will be sure."

Notice who is asking. The sinners in Zion. These are not pagans who never heard God's name. They are people who live in His city, hear His word, and have been living another way underneath it. When God arises, the first trembling is not out there. It is in here. That is an honest verse, and Isaiah does not soften it.

The key word is yagur — dwell, in "Who among us can dwell with the consuming fire?" Yagur does not mean own, or conquer, or drop by for a visit. It means to sojourn — to stay as a guest in a place that is not yours, under the care of a host who receives you. So the question is sharper than it first sounds: who can be God's guest, and live, this close to a fire that consumes? Not who is strong enough to survive Him. Who is welcome enough to stay near Him.

Isaiah's answer is not a feeling and it is not a password. It is a walk, described in plain, everyday verbs. He walks righteously and speaks uprightly. He will not profit from oppression. He shakes a bribe off his hands like something dirty. He stops his ears against schemes of blood and shuts his eyes against enjoying evil. David asked this same question at the door of God's tent and got this same answer: "O LORD, who shall sojourn in your tent? Who shall dwell on your holy hill? He who walks blamelessly and does what is right and speaks truth in his heart" (Psalm 15:1-2). Two witnesses, one door: nearness to a holy God has always had a shape, and the shape is a life.

Then look at the promise at the end. The man who dwells on the heights gets bread given and water sure. Not bread seized. Given. Sure. In a chapter where envoys wept and highways emptied, the safest man in Judah is not the one with the best deal. It is the one whose hands are clean.

Here is the landing for a Saturday. Most of us do not fear being far from God. We fear being close to Him as we actually are. Isaiah says do not solve that by backing away from the fire. Solve it by walking differently in the small, ordinary places the verse names: what we say, what we will not take, what we refuse to listen to, what we will not look at with pleasure. The fire does not change. Guests do. And the ones who walk uprightly do not merely endure the heights — they are fed there.

Lord, make our walk upright and our hands clean, that we may dwell near You without fear, and trust You for bread given and water sure. Amen.

From The Ancient World

Some ancient Christian texts survive as whole books, copied for centuries. This one, if the reading holds, survived as a scrap two inches by three.

Charisma Magazine reported on November 5, 2026, following the Daily Mail, that researchers Marianna Thoma and Amphilochios Papathomas of the University of Athens have deciphered a Greek papyrus fragment, about 1,600 years old, held among unpublished papyri at the Austrian National Library in Vienna. Their study was published in January in the scholarly journal Tyche. They date the fragment to the fourth or fifth century.

What they think it says is striking in its smallness. Their tentative translation reads, in part: "…alone (?) he who descended (into Hades) ascended again (?)…" Keep those question marks — the researchers put them there. Much of the fragment is missing, letters are gone, and parts of the reading are uncertain. In their judgment, the sense is probably this: that only Jesus, and no one else, went down into Hades, the realm of the dead, and returned through His resurrection.

What kind of writing is it? They do not claim a lost gospel, and neither should we. They suggest it may preserve a sermon — perhaps by a church father or an Egyptian religious leader — or a hymn, a work that did not survive through medieval copying. They compared its wording with passages attributed to Eusebius and with hymns about the Savior descending into the regions of death.

That caution is what makes the scrap worth a morning. Scripture already says this plainly — Peter writes of Christ proclaiming to the spirits in prison, and the risen Jesus declares that He holds the keys of Death and Hades. The fragment adds no new Scripture. What it shows is an ancient believer, sixteen centuries ago, preaching or singing about the same descent and rising we still confess — on papyrus small enough to hide in a child's hand.

Source: Charisma Magazine, November 5, 2026, reporting the Daily Mail and the study by Marianna Thoma and Amphilochios Papathomas (University of Athens) in Tyche (January), on a fragment held by the Austrian National Library, Vienna.
